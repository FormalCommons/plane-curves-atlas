# Projective plane curves

This page covers Chapter 5, "Projective Plane Curves", of Fulton's *Algebraic Curves* [@Fulton2008, Chapter 5, pp. 53–66]. It is the centre of the subject: it defines multiplicities and intersection numbers at points of $\mathbb{P}^2$, counts the curves of degree $d$ with assigned multiple points, proves Bézout's theorem and Max Noether's fundamental theorem ("$AF+BG$"), and applies them to Pascal's and Pappus's theorems, to "eight points determine the ninth" on a cubic, and to the group law on a nonsingular cubic.

Sections of the book covered:

- §5.1 Definitions, pp. 53–55 (Problems 5.1–5.16);
- §5.2 Linear systems of curves, pp. 55–57 (Problems 5.17–5.19);
- §5.3 Bézout's theorem, pp. 57–59 (Problems 5.20–5.24, including the Hessian and the Weierstrass form of a cubic);
- §5.4 Multiple points, pp. 59–60 (Problems 5.25–5.28);
- §5.5 Max Noether's fundamental theorem, pp. 60–62 (Problems 5.29–5.30);
- §5.6 Applications of Noether's theorem, pp. 62–65 (Problems 5.31–5.43; p. 66 is blank).

The page relies on [local properties of plane curves](01-local-properties.md) (Chapter 3: multiplicity, tangent lines, affine intersection numbers and their properties (1)–(9)), on [affine varieties](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md) (Chapters 1–2: the Nullstellensatz, forms and (de)homogenization, the decomposition of $k[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I$ for an ideal with finitely many zeros, exact sequences of vector spaces) and on [projective varieties](../auxiliary/projective-varieties.md) (Chapter 4: $\mathbb{P}^n$, homogeneous ideals, projective changes of coordinates, local rings of projective varieties and their identification with affine ones).

## Conventions

### The book

- **Field.** $k$ is algebraically closed from Chapter 3 on (preface). The main text of Chapter 5 (§§5.1–5.6) makes no characteristic assumption, and its proofs work in every characteristic (with the gaps noted under "Notes on the source"). Characteristic $0$ is assumed explicitly in Problems 5.23 (Hessian and flexes), 5.24 (Weierstrass form) and 5.26 (lines through a point); Problem 5.28 is explicitly in characteristic $p$. Several other problems tacitly need $\operatorname{char} k \ne 2$ or $\ne 3$ (5.10, 5.37(a), (d), (e), (f), 5.38, 5.39); each milestone below states the exact hypothesis. Appendix A [@Fulton2008, Appendix A, p. 113] mentions Chapter 5 only for Problems 5.26 and 5.28 ("terrible points").
- **Curves.** A projective plane curve is a nonconstant form $F\in k[X,Y,Z]$ up to a nonzero scalar; its degree is the degree of $F$; multiple components are allowed, and components and their multiplicities are as for affine curves (§3.1). $V(F)$ (the zero set) forgets multiplicities; the book's statements are about forms.
- **Points and charts.** $\mathbb{P}^2=\mathbb{P}^2(k)$ with coordinates $[x:y:z]$. For finitely many points under consideration the book picks a line $L$ missing all of them and sets $F_*=F/L^{\deg F}\in k(\mathbb{P}^2)$, an element of $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)$ for each of the points; changing $L$ multiplies $F_*$ by a unit. After a change of coordinates making $L=Z$, $F_*=F(X,Y,1)$.
- **Changes of coordinates.** For a projective change of coordinates $T=(T_1,T_2,T_3)$ (linear forms), $F^T=F(T_1,T_2,T_3)$ and $V^T=T^{-1}(V)$; so $m_Q(F^T)=m_{T(Q)}(F)$. $F$ and $G$ are projectively equivalent if $G=F^T$ for some $T$.
- **Intersection numbers** take values in $\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}$, with $I(P,F\cap G)=\infty$ exactly when $F$ and $G$ have a common component through $P$.
- **Zero-cycles** are elements of the free abelian group on the set $\mathbb{P}^2$; "$\sum n_PP\ge\sum m_PP$" means $n_P\ge m_P$ for every $P$.
- **Numbering.** In the 2008 edition theorems and propositions are numbered through the chapter, not the section: Theorem 1 is in §5.2, Theorem 2 in §5.4, Proposition 1 in §5.5, Propositions 2–4 in §5.6; corollaries are numbered within their parent result. Bézout's theorem and Max Noether's fundamental theorem are unnumbered. Problems are numbered 5.1–5.43 through the chapter.

### The libraries, and traps

- **Curves as forms.** Recommended representation: a nonzero `F : MvPolynomial (Fin 3) k` with `F.IsHomogeneous d`, with every invariant defined on forms and proved invariant under `F ↦ c • F` ($c\ne0$). Fulton's curves are the case `0 < d`; nonzero constants (`d = 0`) are admitted where the book's arguments produce them (see "Degree-zero curves" below). This is the data Tau Ceti's scheme `projectivePlaneCurve (hG : G.IsHomogeneous d)` and Mathlib's `WeierstrassCurve.Projective.polynomial` (an `MvPolynomial (Fin 3) R`) already use, so results transfer without translation. For §5.2 the space of curves of degree $d$ is the projectivization of the vector space `MvPolynomial.homogeneousSubmodule (Fin 3) k d`; prefer stating linear-system results as `finrank` statements about subspaces (projective dimension $=$ `finrank` $-1$, the empty system being the zero subspace, of dimension $-1$ as in Problem 4.11). Do not model curves as zero sets (multiplicities are lost) or as schemes for the elementary theory (Tau Ceti's scheme is the right target for comparisons, not the working definition). Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.IsHomogeneous.of_mul_left` and `of_mul_right` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/Homogeneous.lean) give that factors of forms are forms, so components are curves.
- **Index order.** Use `Fin 3` with $X=0$, $Y=1$, $Z=2$; this matches Mathlib's Weierstrass files (where `x`, `y`, `z` are notation for `0`, `1`, `2`). Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.dehomogenize i` sets $X_i=1$ and orders the remaining variables by `i.succAbove`: chart $Z=1$ gives $(X,Y)$, chart $Y=1$ gives $(X,Z)$, chart $X=1$ gives $(Y,Z)$.
- **Points.** Use Mathlib's `ℙ k (Fin 3 → k)` (`Projectivization`). Mathlib's Weierstrass files instead use `WeierstrassCurve.Projective.PointClass`, the quotient of *all* of `Fin 3 → R` (zero vector included) by units; the nonsingularity predicate excludes the zero vector. Tau Ceti's `WeierstrassCurve.Projective.Point.toProjectivization` (TauCeti/AlgebraicGeometry/EllipticCurve/Projective/Projectivization.lean) is the comparison.
- **$\mathbb{N}$ versus $\mathbb{N}_\infty$.** Multiplicities and intersection numbers should be `ℕ∞`-valued, matching the book's $\infty$. Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.orderAt` is `ℕ∞`-valued (`⊤` only for the zero polynomial). For intersection numbers use `Module.length` (`ℕ∞`, Mathlib/RingTheory/Length.lean) or `Module.rank`; never `Module.finrank`, whose value on an infinite-dimensional space is the junk value $0$, which would make $I(P,F\cap F)=0$.
- **Linear substitutions.** Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.linearSubst M` satisfies `eval x (linearSubst M p) = eval (M *ᵥ x) p`, so $F^T$ is `linearSubst` of the matrix of $T$; it is a right action (`linearSubst (M * N) = (linearSubst N).comp (linearSubst M)`).
- **Nonsingularity hypotheses.** Tau Ceti's plane-curve files assume nonsingularity in the form `∀ b : Fin 3 → K, b ≠ 0 → aeval b G = 0 → ∃ j, aeval b (pderiv j G) ≠ 0` for an algebraically closed extension `K`. With `K = k` this is the book's "every point is simple", by Problem 5.1.
- **Weierstrass curves.** Mathlib's `W.polynomial` is $Y^2Z+a_1XYZ+a_3YZ^2-(X^3+a_2X^2Z+a_4XZ^2+a_6Z^3)$, valid in every characteristic; the book's $Y^2Z=X^3+aX^2Z+bXZ^2+cZ^3$ (Problem 5.38) is $a_1=a_3=0$, $a_2=a$, $a_4=b$, $a_6=c$, and is nonsingular only if $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$. Mathlib's group has identity the point at infinity $[0:1:0]$, which is a flex; the book allows any base point $O$. Mathlib's points are the $F$-rational nonsingular points over any field $F$; the book works over algebraically closed $k$. Mathlib calls the curve elliptic (`WeierstrassCurve.IsElliptic`) when $\Delta$ is a unit; that this is the book's "nonsingular cubic" needs Tau Ceti's `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.Δ_eq_zero_iff_exists_isSingular` (perfect fields) plus the fact that $[0:1:0]$ is always a simple point.
- **Degree-zero "curves".** The book's curves are nonconstant, but in Noether's theorem the forms $A$, $B$ may be constants, and in its corollary the residual curve $B$ may be a nonzero constant. State those results, and the intersection numbers and cycles they use, for nonzero forms of any degree $d\ge0$ (a nonzero constant $c$ has $I(P,c\cap G)=0$ and $c\bullet F=0$), to avoid case splits; the milestones that speak of "forms of positive degree" are the book's statements about curves.

## Milestones

### §5.1 Definitions

> [!milestone] Multiplicity of a projective plane curve at a point
> Let $F\in k[X,Y,Z]$ be a nonzero form of degree $d$ and $P\in\mathbb{P}^2$, with homogeneous coordinates $v\ne0$. For any index $i$ with $v_i\ne0$, dehomogenize $F$ with respect to $X_i$ and take the multiplicity (Chapter 3) of the resulting affine polynomial at the affine point corresponding to $P$. This number does not depend on $i$ nor on $v$; it is $m_P(F)$. Moreover: $m_P(cF)=m_P(F)$ for $c\in k^\times$; $m_Q(F^T)=m_{T(Q)}(F)$ for a projective change of coordinates $T$; $m_P(F)=0$ iff $F(P)\ne0$; $m_P(FG)=m_P(F)+m_P(G)$, so $m_P(F)=\sum_i e_im_P(F_i)$ if $F=\prod F_i^{e_i}$; $m_P(F)\le d$. $P$ is a simple point of $F$ if $m_P(F)=1$ and a multiple (singular) point if $m_P(F)\ge2$; $F$ is nonsingular if every point of $V(F)$ is simple. No hypothesis on $k$ is needed for $k$-rational points.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.1, p. 53].
>
> Proof routes: The book: the multiplicity of an affine curve at a point depends only on its local ring (§3.2, Theorem 2), and the local rings of a projective variety in the different charts are canonically isomorphic (§4.3). A chart-free route suits Lean better: define $m_P(F)$ as the order of vanishing of $F$ at the vector $v$ in all three variables (the least degree of a nonzero homogeneous component of $F(v+W)$, $W=(W_0,W_1,W_2)$). Scaling $v$ does not change it, by homogeneity. If $v_i=1$, then in $k[[W]]$ one has $F(v+W)=(1+W_i)^d\,F_{(i)}(a+U)$, where $F_{(i)}$ is the dehomogenization, $a$ the affine point, and the two entries of $U$ are power series whose linear parts $W_j-a_jW_i$ are linearly independent; so the order equals the affine multiplicity. Invariance under $T$ is invariance of the order under an invertible linear substitution. This works in every characteristic.
>
> Formalization notes: Use Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.orderAt` for both the affine multiplicity of Chapter 3 and this one, so that the chart comparison is a single lemma; descend to `ℙ k (Fin 3 → k)` with `Projectivization.lift`. Keep the generality "any field, $k$-rational points". Additivity over products is `MvPolynomial.orderAt_mul` (domains). Depends on the multiplicity of affine curves on [local properties](01-local-properties.md).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti **related**: `MvPolynomial.orderAt`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt_mul`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt_pos_iff`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt_le_totalDegree`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt_le_orderAt_aeval` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/OrderAt.lean) give the order of vanishing at a point of affine space and its behaviour under products and substitutions; `TauCeti.dehomogenize` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/Dehomogenization.lean) and `MvPolynomial.linearSubst` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/LinearSubst.lean) give charts and coordinate changes. No multiplicity at a point of $\mathbb{P}^2$.

> [!milestone] Singular points and tangent lines of a projective plane curve via partial derivatives
> Let $F$ be a nonzero form of degree $d\ge1$ and $P=[v]\in\mathbb{P}^2$. (a) $P$ is a multiple point of $F$ if and only if $F(v)=F_X(v)=F_Y(v)=F_Z(v)=0$ (Problem 5.1, starred). This holds in every characteristic; the condition $F(v)=0$ cannot be dropped when $\operatorname{char}k$ divides $d$ (otherwise it follows from Euler's identity). (b) If $P$ is a simple point, the tangent line to $F$ at $P$ is $F_X(v)X+F_Y(v)Y+F_Z(v)Z=0$, a nonzero linear form that vanishes at $P$ (Problem 5.4, starred). (c) A line $L$ through $P$ is tangent to $F$ at $P$ if $I(P,F\cap L)>m_P(F)$ (the book's definition, justified by Problem 3.19); the tangent lines at $P$ are the linear factors of the lowest-degree form of $F$ at $P$ in an affine chart, and $P$ is an ordinary multiple point if there are $m_P(F)$ distinct ones. (d) If $P=[0:1:0]$ and $F=\sum_iF_i(X,Z)Y^{d-i}$ with $F_i$ a form of degree $i$, then $m_P(F)$ is the least $m$ with $F_m\ne0$, and the tangent lines at $P$ are the linear factors of $F_m$ (Problem 5.5, starred).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.1, p. 54], [@Fulton2008, §5.1, Problems 5.1, 5.4, 5.5].
>
> Proof routes: Pass to a chart containing $P$, where "simple" means that an affine partial derivative is nonzero (§3.1) and the tangents are the factors of the lowest form; recover the missing partial derivative from Euler's identity $XF_X+YF_Y+ZF_Z=dF$. For (d), dehomogenize at $Y=1$.
>
> Formalization notes: With the chart-free multiplicity of the previous milestone, a chart-free definition of tangent lines is available: the lowest homogeneous component of $F(v+W)$ is a form in $W$ that is invariant under $W\mapsto W+tv$, and its linear factors are exactly the linear forms vanishing at $P$ that are tangent to $F$ at $P$; in degree one it is $\sum_iF_{X_i}(v)W_i$, which is (b). Prove the book's characterization (c) from property (5) of intersection numbers. Define a predicate `IsNonsingular F` as "every nonzero $v$ with $F(v)=0$ has a nonzero partial derivative": (a) identifies it with the book's notion, and it is literally the hypothesis of Tau Ceti's plane-curve theorems. Depends on: Multiplicity of a projective plane curve at a point; tangent lines and Problem 3.19 on [local properties](01-local-properties.md).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `MvPolynomial.IsHomogeneous.sum_X_mul_pderiv` (Mathlib/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/EulerIdentity.lean) is Euler's identity. Tau Ceti **partial**: `TauCeti.forall_eval_pderiv_eq_zero_iff_dehomogenize`, `TauCeti.exists_eval_pderiv_ne_zero_iff_dehomogenize` and `TauCeti.eval_pderiv_add_sum_dehomogenize` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/Dehomogenization.lean) prove, in every characteristic, that at a zero of a form the projective gradient vanishes iff the gradient in a chart does, which is the gradient half of (a); `MvPolynomial.orderAt_eq_one_of_eval_pderiv_ne_zero` and `MvPolynomial.homogeneousComponent_one_taylor` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/OrderAt.lean) relate order one and the linear Taylor part to the gradient. The link with $m_P$, (b) as a statement about tangent lines, (c) and (d) are absent.

> [!milestone] Local ring of an irreducible projective plane curve as a quotient and in affine charts
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $F$ an irreducible form of degree $n\ge1$, $C=V(F)$ and $P\in C$. Let $k(C)$ and $\mathcal{O}_P(C)\subseteq k(C)$ be as Fulton defines them (§4.2; the representation is fixed on the [front page](README.md)): the quotients $f/g$ of forms of equal degree in $\Gamma_h(F)=k[X,Y,Z]/(F)$ with $g\ne0$, and those among them with $g(P)\ne0$. Let $L$ be a linear form with $L(P)\ne0$ and $F_*=F/L^n\in\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)$.
>
> (a) Sending $G/H$ ($G,H$ forms of equal degree, $H(P)\ne0$) to $\bar G/\bar H$, bars denoting residues in $\Gamma_h(F)$, is a surjective $k$-algebra homomorphism $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)\to\mathcal{O}_P(C)$ with kernel $F_*\,\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)$, an ideal that does not depend on $L$. So $\mathcal{O}_P(C)\cong\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*)$, and for every form $G$, $\mathcal{O}_P(C)/(\bar G_*)\cong\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*,G_*)$.
>
> (b) Let $X_i$ be a coordinate with $X_i(P)\ne0$, $F_{(i)}\in k[Y_0,Y_1]$ the dehomogenization of $F$ with respect to $X_i$, and $P_{(i)}\in\mathbb{A}^2$ the point of the chart corresponding to $P$. Then $F_{(i)}$ is irreducible of degree $n$, and dehomogenization ($f/g\mapsto f_{(i)}/g_{(i)}$, the book's $\alpha$ of §4.3) induces $k$-algebra isomorphisms $k(C)\cong k(F_{(i)})=\operatorname{Frac}\bigl(k[Y_0,Y_1]/(F_{(i)})\bigr)$ and $\mathcal{O}_P(C)\cong\mathcal{O}_{P_{(i)}}(F_{(i)})\cong\mathcal{O}_{P_{(i)}}(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F_{(i)})$, compatible with (a) and with the chart isomorphism $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)\cong\mathcal{O}_{P_{(i)}}(\mathbb{A}^2)$. For $P=[x:y:1]$ this is the book's $\mathcal{O}_P(F)\cong\mathcal{O}_{(x,y)}(F_*)$.
>
> For every nonzero form $F$ of positive degree (reducible, or with multiple components) and every $P$ with $X_i(P)\ne0$, the chart isomorphism still gives $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*)\cong\mathcal{O}_{P_{(i)}}(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F_{(i)})$; this is the ring in which Chapters 3 and 5 compute for such curves (both are $0$ if $P\notin V(F)$).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.1, p. 53] ($\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ canonically isomorphic to $\mathcal{O}_{(x,y)}(F_*)$ for $P=[x:y:1]$; the residue $\bar G_*$ of $G_*$ in $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$); [@Fulton2008, §5.5, proof of Proposition 1, p. 62] ($\mathcal{O}_P(F)/(\bar G_*)\cong\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*,G_*)$, citing Problem 2.44); [@Fulton2008, §4.3, p. 49] (charts and local rings).
>
> Proof routes: (b) The chart milestone of the projective page applied to $V=C$ (its $V_*$ in the chart is $V(F_{(i)})$, and $I(C)=(F)$ by the projective Nullstellensatz, so $I(C)_*=(F_{(i)})$), then Problem 2.44 (starred) for $\mathcal{O}_{P_{(i)}}(F_{(i)})\cong\mathcal{O}_{P_{(i)}}(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F_{(i)})$. $F_{(i)}$ is irreducible of degree $n$ because $X_i\nmid F$ (otherwise $C=V(X_i)$ would not contain $P$), by the Corollary to Proposition 5 of §2.6. >

> [!milestone] Intersection number of projective plane curves at a point
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $F,G$ forms of positive degree and $P\in\mathbb{P}^2$. Define $I(P,F\cap G)=\dim_k\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*,G_*)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}$, where $F_*=F/L^{\deg F}$, $G_*=G/L^{\deg G}$ for a linear form $L$ with $L(P)\ne0$. This does not depend on $L$, nor on scaling $F$ or $G$, and equals the affine intersection number (Chapter 3) of the dehomogenizations of $F$ and $G$ in any standard chart containing $P$. It satisfies: (1) $I(P,F\cap G)\in\mathbb{N}$ if $F$ and $G$ have no common component through $P$, and $=\infty$ otherwise; (2) $I(P,F\cap G)=0$ iff $P\notin V(F)\cap V(G)$, and it depends only on the components of $F$ and $G$ through $P$; (3) $I(P,F\cap G)=I(Q,F^T\cap G^T)$ for a projective change of coordinates $T$ with $T(Q)=P$; (4) $I(P,F\cap G)=I(P,G\cap F)$; (5) $I(P,F\cap G)\ge m_P(F)m_P(G)$, with equality iff $F$ and $G$ have no tangent line in common at $P$; (6) if $F=\prod F_i^{r_i}$ and $G=\prod G_j^{s_j}$ then $I(P,F\cap G)=\sum_{i,j}r_is_jI(P,F_i\cap G_j)$; (7) $I(P,F\cap G)=I(P,F\cap(G+AF))$ for every form $A$ of degree $\deg G-\deg F$ (only $A=0$ if $\deg G<\deg F$); (8) if $P$ is a simple point of $F$, then $I(P,F\cap G)=\operatorname{ord}^F_P(G)$, where for $F$ irreducible $\operatorname{ord}^F_P(G)$ is the order at $P$ of the rational function $G/H$ on $F$, for any form $H$ of degree $\deg G$ with $H(P)\ne0$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.1, pp. 53–54].
>
> Proof routes: The book reduces everything to a chart and to §3.3, using the canonical identification of $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)$ with the local ring of the chart (§4.3) and the fact that changing $L$ multiplies $F_*$ by a unit. Lean design (the one the [front page](README.md) and the projective page fix): $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)$ is Mathlib's `HomogeneousLocalization.AtPrime` at the homogeneous prime of $P$, and $I(P,F\cap G)$ is the `Module.length` of its quotient by the ideal generated by $F_*$ and $G_*$, which does not depend on $L$. > [!milestone] Finiteness of the intersection of two projective plane curves
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed. (a) If the forms $F,G$ of positive degree have no common component, then $V(F)\cap V(G)$ is finite (Problem 5.7, starred). (b) If $F$ is irreducible, then at least one of $F_X,F_Y,F_Z$ is nonzero, and $F$ has only finitely many multiple points (Problem 5.8, starred). Both hold in every characteristic. Conversely, any two projective plane curves meet: $V(F)\cap V(G)\ne\emptyset$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.1, Problems 5.7, 5.8].
>
> Proof routes: (a) In each of the three charts the dehomogenizations have no common factor (factoring a form is factoring its dehomogenization up to powers of the variable, §2.6, Proposition 5 and its corollary), so §1.6, Proposition 2 gives finitely many common zeros. (b) If all partial derivatives vanish, then $F$ is constant (characteristic $0$) or $F\in k[X^p,Y^p,Z^p]$, hence $F=G^p$ since $k$ is perfect, contradicting irreducibility; a nonzero partial derivative has degree $<\deg F$, so is not divisible by $F$, and (a) applies to $F$ and it, with Problem 5.1. That two curves meet is the projective dimension theorem; Bézout's theorem also gives it.
>
> Formalization notes: (a) is used to move all intersection points off a line in the proofs of Bézout and Noether; state it as `Set.Finite`. (b) is used in Problem 5.22 and Theorem 2. Depends on: Singular points and tangent lines via partial derivatives; §1.6 and §2.6 on [affine varieties](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md).
>

### §5.2 Linear systems of curves

> [!milestone] Dimension of linear systems of plane curves with assigned multiple points
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $d\ge1$ (the statements before (a) hold over any field). The forms of degree $d$ in $X,Y,Z$ form a vector space of dimension $(d+1)(d+2)/2$ with the monomials as basis, so the curves of degree $d$ are the points of $\mathbb{P}^N$, $N=d(d+3)/2$ (lines form a $\mathbb{P}^2$, conics a $\mathbb{P}^5$, cubics a $\mathbb{P}^9$, quartics a $\mathbb{P}^{14}$). (Lemma) For a fixed point $P$, the curves of degree $d$ through $P$ form a hyperplane of $\mathbb{P}^N$; for a projective change of coordinates $T$ of $\mathbb{P}^2$, $F\mapsto F^T$ is a projective change of coordinates of $\mathbb{P}^N$. Hence the curves of degree $d$ through a given set of points form a linear subvariety (a linear system), and through any $d(d+3)/2$ points there passes a curve of degree $d$. (a) For a point $P$ and an integer $0\le r\le d+1$, the forms $F$ of degree $d$ with $m_P(F)\ge r$ (together with $0$) form a subspace of codimension $r(r+1)/2$ in the space of forms of degree $d$. (b) (Theorem 1 (1)) For distinct points $P_1,\dots,P_n$ and integers $r_i\ge0$, $V(d;r_1P_1,\dots,r_nP_n)=\{F$ of degree $d: m_{P_i}(F)\ge r_i\}$ is a linear subvariety of $\mathbb{P}^{d(d+3)/2}$ of dimension at least $d(d+3)/2-\sum_ir_i(r_i+1)/2$ (dimension $-1$ meaning empty). (c) (Theorem 1 (2)) If $d\ge(\sum_ir_i)-1$, the dimension is exactly $d(d+3)/2-\sum_ir_i(r_i+1)/2$, i.e. the conditions are independent.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.2, Lemma, pp. 55–56], [@Fulton2008, §5.2, p. 56 and Theorem 1, pp. 56–57].
>
> Proof routes: Space of curves and Lemma: Monomial basis (Problem 2.35); evaluation at a representative of $P$ is a nonzero linear functional on forms because some monomial is nonzero at $P$; $F\mapsto F^T$ is linear and invertible with inverse $F\mapsto F^{T^{-1}}$; $N$ hyperplanes of $\mathbb{P}^N$ meet (Problem 4.12), i.e. $N$ linear conditions on an $(N+1)$-dimensional space leave a nonzero solution. (a) Move $P$ to $[0:0:1]$; $m_P(F)\ge r$ iff the coefficients of the monomials $X^iY^jZ^l$ with $i+j<r$ vanish (Problem 5.5), and there are $r(r+1)/2$ of them. (b) Intersect. (c) The book: induction on $\sum r_i-1$. If all $r_i=1$, the form $L_1\cdots L_{n-1}L_0^{d-n+1}$ (lines $L_i$ through $P_i$ avoiding the other points, $L_0$ avoiding all, Problem 5.14) shows each new point imposes a new condition; if some $r=r_1>1$, refine $V(d;(r-1)P_1,\dots)\supset V(d;rP_1,\dots)$ through $r$ coefficient conditions and show each step is strict using forms $YF_i$, $XF_{r-2}$ built from the degree $d-1$ case. A route that works over any field for rational points, with a triangular family of forms instead of an induction: in local coordinates at $P_i$ given by two independent lines $M_1,M_2$ through $P_i$, the condition "coefficient of $x^ay^b$ vanishes" ($a+b<r_i$) is violated by $M_1^aM_2^b\prod_{j\ne i}L_j^{r_j}\,L_0^{e}$, with $L_j$ a line through $P_j$ missing $P_i$, $L_0$ a line missing $P_i$ and $e=d-a-b-\sum_{j\ne i}r_j\ge0$ exactly because $d\ge\sum_jr_j-1$; this form satisfies every condition at the other points and every condition at $P_i$ of degree $\le a+b$ other than that one, so ordering the conditions by degree shows they are independent (all these lines exist over any field).
>
> Formalization notes: Work with subspaces of `homogeneousSubmodule (Fin 3) k d` and `finrank`; the projective language is a wrapper. State in the vector space: `finrank` of the subspace $=\dim-\sum r_i(r_i+1)/2$ under the hypothesis of (c), and $\ge$ in general. Note that (a) for $r=d+1$ gives the zero subspace. Theorem 1 is used in §5.4 (only (b), with $d=n-1$) and again in Chapters 7–8 (adjoint curves). Depends on: Multiplicity of a projective plane curve at a point; Problem 5.14; linear subvarieties (Problem 4.11) and Problem 4.12 on [projective varieties](../auxiliary/projective-varieties.md).
>

Tau Ceti **partial** (the dimension count before the Lemma only): `TauCeti.finrank_homogeneousSubmodule_eq_multichoose` (TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/Finrank.lean) gives the dimension of the space of forms of degree $d$ in $\#\sigma$ variables as a multichoose number ($=(d+1)(d+2)/2$ for three variables); `TauCeti.veroneseLinearForm` and `Projectivization.veronese` (TauCeti/LinearAlgebra/Projectivization/Veronese.lean) present a form of degree $d$ as a linear form in the degree-$d$ monomials, so that "$F(P)=0$" is a hyperplane condition on the coefficients. ### §5.3 Bézout's theorem

> [!milestone] Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $F,G$ forms of degrees $m,n\ge1$ with no common component ($F$ and $G$ may be reducible and have multiple components). Then $\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^2}I(P,F\cap G)=mn$, the sum having finitely many nonzero terms. In particular (Problem 5.12) a line $L$ that is not a component of $F$ satisfies $\sum_PI(P,F\cap L)=\deg F$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.3, Bézout's Theorem, pp. 57–58], [@Fulton2008, §5.1, Problem 5.12].
>
> Proof routes: The book: after a projective change of coordinates no point of $V(F)\cap V(G)$ lies on $Z=0$ (finiteness and a line missing finitely many points, Problem 4.25). Then $\sum_PI(P,F\cap G)=\dim_kk[X,Y]/(F_*,G_*)$ with $F_*=F(X,Y,1)$ (property (9) of §3.3, from §2.9, Proposition 6). With $R=k[X,Y,Z]$ and $\Gamma=R/(F,G)$: Step 1, the exact sequence $0\to R_{d-m-n}\to R_{d-m}\times R_{d-n}\to R_d\to\Gamma_d\to0$, $C\mapsto(GC,-FC)$, $(A,B)\mapsto AF+BG$ (exactness in the middle uses that $F,G$ are coprime in the UFD $R$), gives $\dim\Gamma_d=mn$ for $d\ge m+n$ (the binomial computation in fact works for $d\ge m+n-2$); Step 2, multiplication by $Z$ is injective on $\Gamma$ (because $F(X,Y,0)$ and $G(X,Y,0)$ are coprime); Step 3, for $d\ge m+n$ the residues of a basis of $\Gamma_d$, dehomogenized, form a basis of $k[X,Y]/(F_*,G_*)$. Alternatives: the resultant $\operatorname{Res}_Y(F,G)$, a form of degree $mn$ in $X,Z$ whose root multiplicities are the intersection numbers in general coordinates (harder to formalize: the multiplicity comparison is local algebra again); or a scheme-theoretic route through degrees of line bundles on the curve $G=0$.
>
> Formalization notes: Make Steps 1 and 2 standalone lemmas; Max Noether's fundamental theorem reuses Step 2. Step 2 says that $(F,G)$ is saturated with respect to $Z$, i.e. equals the homogenization of $(F_*,G_*)$; Tau Ceti's `Ideal.homogenization` API (`Ideal.X_mul_mem_homogenization_iff`, `Ideal.mk_X_mem_nonZeroDivisors_homogenization`, `Ideal.homogenizationAwayEquiv`) packages exactly this and Step 3. Use a `Finset` of intersection points or `finsum` for the sum. A version over an arbitrary field (closed points weighted by residue degrees, or points over $\bar k$) is natural but not needed for the book. Depends on: Intersection number of projective plane curves at a point; Finiteness of the intersection of two projective plane curves; property (9) on [local properties](01-local-properties.md); §2.6, Proposition 5, §2.9, Proposition 6 and §2.10, Proposition 7 on [affine varieties](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md); Problem 4.25 on [projective varieties](../auxiliary/projective-varieties.md).
>

> [!milestone] Corollaries of Bézout's theorem on multiplicities and common points
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $F,G$ forms of degrees $m,n\ge1$. (Corollary 1) If $F$ and $G$ have no common component, then $\sum_Pm_P(F)m_P(G)\le mn$. (Corollary 2) If $V(F)\cap V(G)$ consists of exactly $mn$ distinct points, then each of them is a simple point of $F$ and of $G$ (and $F$, $G$ meet transversally there). (Corollary 3) If $V(F)\cap V(G)$ has more than $mn$ points, then $F$ and $G$ have a common component.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.3, Corollaries 1–3, p. 58].
>
> Proof routes: Bézout's theorem with property (5) of intersection numbers; for Corollary 2, $mn$ points each with $I\ge1$ force $I=1$, hence $m_P(F)m_P(G)=1$; for Corollary 3, finitely many points (no common component) with $I\ge1$ each sum to at most $mn$.
>
> Formalization notes: Corollary 2 needs no explicit "no common component" hypothesis: a common component would give infinitely many common points. Depends on: Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves.
>

> [!milestone] Nonsingular projective plane curves are irreducible
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $F$ a nonsingular form of degree $d\ge1$ (every point of $V(F)$ simple). Then $F$ is irreducible; in particular it has no multiple component. The affine analogue fails: $X(X-1)$ defines a nonsingular reducible affine curve (Problem 5.21 asks this).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.3, Problem 5.21].
>
> Proof routes: If $F=GH$ with $G,H$ nonconstant, then $G$ and $H$ meet in some point $P$ (two plane curves always meet), and $m_P(F)=m_P(G)+m_P(H)\ge2$. Tau Ceti's proof is the same, with the dimension theorem (Krull's height theorem) supplying the common point, in $\mathbb{P}^n$ for $n\ge2$.
>
> Formalization notes: Tau Ceti's statement is more general (hypersurfaces in $\mathbb{P}^n$, $n\ge2$, any field $k$, nonsingularity tested over an algebraically closed extension, giving absolute irreducibility). The library only needs the bridge from its `IsNonsingular` predicate (via Problem 5.1) to Tau Ceti's hypothesis. Used in: the group law on a nonsingular cubic (a nonsingular cubic is irreducible, as Proposition 3 requires).
>

> [!milestone] Polar bound on the multiple points of an irreducible plane curve
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $F$ irreducible of degree $n\ge2$ with $F_X\ne0$ (true after a change of coordinates, Problem 5.8). Then $\sum_Pm_P(F)\,(m_P(F)-1)\le n(n-1)$; in particular $F$ has at most $n(n-1)/2$ multiple points. Every characteristic. The key local input (Problem 5.6, starred) is $m_P(F_X)\ge m_P(F)-1$ for $P\in V(F)$ (with $m_P(0)=\infty$), also characteristic-free.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.3, Problem 5.22], [@Fulton2008, §5.1, Problem 5.6].
>
> Proof routes: Corollary 1 of Bézout for $F$ and $F_X$, which have no common component because $F$ is irreducible and $0\ne F_X$ has smaller degree; then Problem 5.6. Problem 5.6: differentiate the Taylor expansion in a chart (for $P$ in the chart $Z=1$, $F_X$ dehomogenizes to the $X$-derivative of the dehomogenization; likewise in the chart $Y=1$). The point $[1:0:0]$ lies in neither chart; the chart-free multiplicity covers every point at once, since $F_X(v+W)=\partial_{W_0}\big(F(v+W)\big)$ and differentiation lowers the order by at most one.
>

> [!milestone] Hessian criterion for flexes and the nine flexes of a nonsingular cubic
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed of characteristic $0$ (the book's hypothesis; it suffices that $\operatorname{char}k\nmid2(n-1)$ for Theorem (1) and Corollary (1), and $\operatorname{char}k\nmid6(n-1)$ for Theorem (2) and Corollary (2), see the notes) and $F$ a form of degree $n$ with no line as a component. A simple point $P$ of $F$ with tangent line $T$ is a flex if $I(P,F\cap T)\ge3$, an ordinary flex if $=3$ (Problem 3.12). Let $H=\det(\partial^2F/\partial X_i\partial X_j)$, the Hessian, a form of degree $3(n-2)$ or $0$. (Theorem) (1) A point $P\in V(F)$ lies on $V(H)$ iff $P$ is a flex or a multiple point of $F$. (2) $I(P,H\cap F)=1$ iff $P$ is an ordinary flex. (Corollary) (1) A nonsingular curve of degree $n\ge3$ has a flex. (2) A nonsingular cubic has exactly nine flexes, all ordinary.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.3, Problem 5.23, Theorem and Corollary, p. 59].
>
> Proof routes: The book's outline: (a) the Hessian of $F^T$ is $(\det T)^2H^T$, so one may take $P=[0:0:1]$; (b) Euler's identity $(n-1)F_j=\sum_iX_iF_{ij}$; (c) row and column operations give $I(P,f\cap h)=I(P,f\cap g)$ with $g=f_y^2f_{xx}+f_x^2f_{yy}-2f_xf_yf_{xy}$ in the chart; (d) at a multiple point $I(P,f\cap g)>1$; (e) at a simple point with tangent $y=0$, $f=y+ax^2+bxy+cy^2+dx^3+ex^2y+\dots$, one has $g=2a+6dx+(8ac-2b^2+2e)y+\dots$, and $P$ is a flex iff $a=0$, ordinary iff moreover $d\ne0$. Step (c) rests on the identity $h=n(n-1)f\,(f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2)-(n-1)^2g$ (with $h=H(x,y,1)$), which holds over $\mathbb{Z}$ and was checked symbolically here for $n=2,3,4$; so $I(P,f\cap h)=I(P,f\cap g)$ once $n-1$ is invertible. Corollary (1): if $H$ is a nonzero form of degree $3(n-2)>0$ it meets $F$, and if $H=0$ every point is on it; either way (1) of the Theorem gives a flex, with no need for $H\ne0$. Corollary (2): every flex of a cubic is ordinary (a line meets a cubic with total multiplicity $3$), so if $F$ divided $H$ every point of $F$ would be an ordinary flex with $I(P,H\cap F)=\infty$, contradicting (2); hence $F\nmid H$, Bézout gives $\sum_PI(P,H\cap F)=9$, and each flex contributes exactly $1$. Alternative for the count, once one flex is known: with a flex $O$ as base point, the flexes are the points with $3P=O$ (Problem 5.37) and, through the Weierstrass bridge, Tau Ceti's structure theorem for $3$-torsion gives nine of them whenever $\operatorname{char}k\ne3$.
>
> Formalization notes: Checked here: the outline uses the characteristic only through $(n-1)^2$ in (c), $2a$ in (e) and $6d$ in (e). Hence Theorem (1) and Corollary (1) hold whenever $\operatorname{char}k\nmid2(n-1)$, and Theorem (2) and Corollary (2) whenever $\operatorname{char}k\nmid6(n-1)$ (for cubics: $\operatorname{char}k\ne2,3$); state them so, with the book's characteristic $0$ as a special case. In characteristic $2$, $H=0$ for every form: each $F_{X_iX_i}$ vanishes (the coefficients $a(a-1)$ are even), and a symmetric $3\times3$ matrix with zero diagonal has determinant $2F_{XY}F_{XZ}F_{YZ}$. Yet a nonsingular cubic in characteristic $2$ is not all flexes (it has nine). In characteristic $3$ a nonsingular cubic has $1$ or $3$ flexes, so (2) fails there. Corollary (2) needs $F\nmid H$ for Bézout; for a cubic this follows from Theorem (2), as shown above, so no extra input is needed (see "Notes on the source"). Depends on: Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves; Singular points and tangent lines via partial derivatives; flexes (Problem 3.12) on [local properties](01-local-properties.md).

> [!milestone] Projective normal forms of irreducible conics and cubics (Weierstrass form)
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed. (a) Every irreducible conic is nonsingular and projectively equivalent to $YZ=X^2$ (Problem 5.9; every characteristic). (b) An irreducible cubic is either nonsingular or has exactly one singular point, which is a double point, a node or a cusp (Problem 5.13; every characteristic). (c) If $O$ is a flex of an irreducible cubic $F$, there is a projective change of coordinates moving $O$ to $[0:1:0]$ and its tangent to $Z=0$, after which $F$ is a nonzero multiple of a Weierstrass cubic $Y^2Z+a_1XYZ+a_3YZ^2-(X^3+a_2X^2Z+a_4XZ^2+a_6Z^3)$ (every characteristic); if $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$ one may further reach $Y^2Z=$ a cubic form in $X,Z$ (Problem 5.24 (a), stated in characteristic $0$). (d) In characteristic $0$ (the book's hypothesis) every irreducible cubic is projectively equivalent to one of $Y^2Z=X^3$ (cusp; Problem 5.10), $Y^2Z=X^2(X+Z)$ (node; equivalently $XYZ=X^3+Y^3$, Problem 5.11), or $Y^2Z=X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z)$ with $\lambda\ne0,1$ (nonsingular) (Problem 5.24 (b)).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.1, Problems 5.9, 5.10, 5.11, 5.13], [@Fulton2008, §5.3, Problem 5.24].
>
> Proof routes: (a) put a simple point at $[0:1:0]$ with tangent $Z=0$ and normalize. (b) Theorem 2 of §5.4 (or a line through two singular points meets the cubic with multiplicity $\ge4$, Problem 5.12). (c) The flex and its tangent force the coefficients of $Y^3$, $XY^2$, $X^2Y$ to vanish and the coefficients of $Y^2Z$ and $X^3$ to be nonzero; rescale $X$ and $Y$. Completing the square in $Y$ needs $2$ invertible. ### §5.4 Multiple points

> [!milestone] Bound on the multiple points of an irreducible plane curve
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $F$ irreducible of degree $n\ge1$. Then $\sum_P\frac{m_P(F)(m_P(F)-1)}{2}\le\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$ (Theorem 2). Consequences: lines and irreducible conics are nonsingular; an irreducible cubic has at most one singular point, a double point; an irreducible quartic has at most three double points, or one triple point and no other singular point. The bound is sharp: $X^n+Y^{n-1}Z$ is irreducible with $[0:0:1]$ of multiplicity $n-1$. Every characteristic. Generalization (Problem 5.25): if $F$ has no multiple components and $c$ irreducible components, then $\sum_Pm_P(m_P-1)/2\le(n-1)(n-2)/2+c-1\le n(n-1)/2$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.4, Theorem 2 and the remarks after it, p. 60], [@Fulton2008, §5.4, Problem 5.25].
>
> Proof routes: Let $r=\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{2}-\sum_P\frac{m_P(m_P-1)}{2}$; by the polar bound $r\ge0$. Choose $r$ simple points $Q_1,\dots,Q_r$ of $F$ (an irreducible curve has infinitely many points and finitely many multiple ones). By Theorem 1 (1) of §5.2 with $d=n-1$ there is a curve $G$ of degree $n-1$ with $m_P(G)\ge m_P(F)-1$ at every multiple point $P$ and through every $Q_i$. Corollary 1 of Bézout ($F$ irreducible of larger degree, so no common component) gives $n(n-1)\ge\sum_Pm_P(m_P-1)+r$; substitute $r$.
>
> Formalization notes: The sum is over the finitely many multiple points. Problem 5.25 is the natural general form (reduced curves); prove it by splitting points on one component and on two (its hint), after Theorem 2. In Chapter 8 the difference of the two sides becomes the genus of an irreducible curve with ordinary multiple points; Tau Ceti's genus of a nonsingular plane curve is the case with no multiple points. Depends on: Polar bound on the multiple points of an irreducible plane curve; Dimension of linear systems of plane curves with assigned multiple points; Corollaries of Bézout's theorem on multiplicities and common points.
>

> [!milestone] Generic lines through a point meet an irreducible curve in distinct points
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $F$ irreducible of degree $n$, $P\in\mathbb{P}^2$ and $r=m_P(F)\ge0$. Assume $\operatorname{char}k=0$, or more generally that $n-r$ is nonzero in $k$, or that $n=r$ (then $F$ is a line through $P$ and the statement is trivial). Then for all but finitely many lines $L$ through $P$, $L$ meets $V(F)$ in exactly $n-r$ points other than $P$, each with $I(Q,F\cap L)=1$ (and $I(P,F\cap L)=r$). (Problem 5.26, starred, stated in characteristic $0$; Problem 5.27 extends it to $F$ without multiple components.) In characteristic $p$ it can fail when $p\mid n-r$: for $F=X^{p+1}-Y^pZ$ and $P=[0:1:0]$ every line tangent to $F$ at a simple point passes through $P$ (Problem 5.28); Appendix A calls such points terrible and argues, through the dual curve (Problem 6.47), that each $F$ has at most one; the milestone [Terrible points and lines meeting a plane curve in n distinct points](03-resolution-of-singularities.md#74-quadratic-transformations) on the resolution page states this precisely and proves it. So the hypothesis is sufficient but not necessary: by Bézout the conclusion holds exactly at the points that are not terrible, and by that milestone these are all but at most one point $P$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.4, Problems 5.26–5.28, p. 60], [@Fulton2008, Appendix A, p. 113].
>
> Proof routes: The book's outline: take $P=[0:1:0]$, lines $L_\lambda=V(X-\lambda Z)$, $F=A_r(X,Z)Y^{n-r}+\dots+A_n(X,Z)$ with $A_r\ne0$; $G_\lambda(t)=F(\lambda,t,1)$ has $n-r$ distinct roots if $A_r(\lambda,1)\ne0$ and $V(F)\cap V(F_Y)\cap L_\lambda\subseteq\{P\}$. The second condition excludes finitely many $\lambda$ because $F_Y\ne0$ (its $Y^{n-r-1}$ coefficient is $(n-r)A_r$, nonzero when $\operatorname{char}k\nmid n-r$), so $F\nmid F_Y$ and $V(F)\cap V(F_Y)$ is finite.
>
> Formalization notes: Starred: Chapter 7 uses it to choose coordinates for quadratic transformations (Lemma 1 of §7.4, which Appendix A says is false in characteristic $p$ without care). State it with the hypothesis "$(n-r)\cdot1_k\ne0$ or $n=r$", which is what the proof uses (a literal "$\operatorname{char}k\nmid n-r$" would wrongly exclude $n=r$, since everything divides $0$). Where the later chapters need the conclusion in characteristic $p$, they use the terrible-point milestone instead. Depends on: Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves (Problem 5.12); Finiteness of the intersection of two projective plane curves.
>

### §5.5 Max Noether's fundamental theorem

> [!milestone] Max Noether's fundamental theorem
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed. *Zero-cycles and intersection cycles.* A zero-cycle is a finitely supported function $\mathbb{P}^2\to\mathbb{Z}$, written $\sum n_PP$; its degree is $\sum n_P$; it is positive if all $n_P\ge0$; $\sum n_PP\ge\sum m_PP$ means $n_P\ge m_P$ for all $P$. For forms $F,G$ with no common component, $F\bullet G=\sum_PI(P,F\cap G)P$. Then $F\bullet G$ is positive of degree $\deg F\cdot\deg G$ (Bézout), $F\bullet G=G\bullet F$, $F\bullet GH=F\bullet G+F\bullet H$, and $F\bullet(G+AF)=F\bullet G$ for every form $A$ of degree $\deg G-\deg F$.
>
> *The theorem.* Let $F,G$ be forms of positive degree with no common component, and $H$ a form. For $P\in\mathbb{P}^2$, Noether's conditions hold at $P$ (with respect to $F,G,H$) if $H_*\in(F_*,G_*)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)$, all three formed with the same linear form $L$, $L(P)\ne0$ (the condition does not depend on $L$, and holds automatically if $P\notin V(F)\cap V(G)$). Then $H=AF+BG$ for forms $A,B$ of degrees $\deg H-\deg F$ and $\deg H-\deg G$ (a form of negative degree meaning $0$) if and only if Noether's conditions hold at every point of $V(F)\cap V(G)$. Equivalently, $H\in(F,G)\subseteq k[X,Y,Z]$ iff Noether's conditions hold everywhere.
>
> *Consequence* (Problem 5.30). If $F$ is irreducible and $z\in k(F)$ lies in $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ for every $P\in V(F)$, then $z\in k$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.5, pp. 60–61] (zero-cycles, $F\bullet G$ and its properties), [@Fulton2008, §5.5, Max Noether's Fundamental Theorem, p. 61], [@Fulton2008, §5.5, Problem 5.30, p. 62].
>
> Proof routes: One direction is immediate. Conversely, as in Bézout's proof assume $V(F,G,Z)=\emptyset$ and dehomogenize at $Z$. The local conditions say that the image of $H_*$ in $\prod_P\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F_*,G_*)\cong k[X,Y]/(F_*,G_*)$ (§2.9, Proposition 6) vanishes, so $H_*=aF_*+bG_*$ in $k[X,Y]$; homogenizing gives $Z^rH=AF+BG$; Step 2 of Bézout's proof (multiplication by $Z$ is injective modulo $(F,G)$) removes $Z^r$; finally take homogeneous components of $A$ and $B$. In Tau Ceti's language: $(F,G)$ equals the homogenization of $(F_*,G_*)$, and membership in a homogenization is membership of the dehomogenization. The properties of $F\bullet G$ are Properties (4), (6), (7) of intersection numbers and Bézout's theorem. Problem 5.30, as its hint says: write $z=\bar H/\bar G$ with forms $H,G$ of equal degree and $F\nmid G$; at each $P\in V(F)\cap V(G)$, $z\in\mathcal{O}_P(F)\cong\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*)$ gives $H_*\in(F_*,G_*)$, so Noether's theorem gives $H=AF+BG$ with $\deg B=0$, and $z=B$.
>
> Formalization notes: Zero-cycles: Mathlib's `Finsupp`, `ℙ k (Fin 3 → k) →₀ ℤ` (and `→₀ ℕ` for positive ones); Finsupp's pointwise order is the book's "bigger than". Allow nonzero constant forms, with $c\bullet F=0$. State the ideal-membership form (`H ∈ Ideal.span {F, G}`) as the main theorem and derive the degree form from homogeneous components. Express Noether's conditions with the same local ring as the intersection number. Depends on: Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves (Step 2 as a lemma); Finiteness of the intersection of two projective plane curves; Intersection number of projective plane curves at a point; for Problem 5.30, Local ring of an irreducible projective plane curve as a quotient and in affine charts; §2.9, Proposition 6 and §2.6, Proposition 5 on [affine varieties](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md). Used in: §5.6, and in Chapter 7 (§7.5 criterion at ordinary multiple points) and Chapter 8 (residue theorem).
>

> [!milestone] Local criteria for Noether's conditions and the residual intersection corollary
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $F,G,H$ forms, $P\in V(F)\cap V(G)$, with $F,G$ having no common component through $P$. (Proposition 1) Noether's conditions hold at $P$ if (1) $F$ and $G$ meet transversally at $P$ and $H(P)=0$; or (2) $P$ is a simple point of $F$ and $I(P,H\cap F)\ge I(P,G\cap F)$ (the left side may be $\infty$); or (3) $F$ and $G$ have no common tangent at $P$ and $m_P(H)\ge m_P(F)+m_P(G)-1$. For fixed $F,G,P$, the conditions on $H$ in (1) and (2) are also necessary, those in (3) are not (Problem 5.29). (Corollary) Let $F,G,H$ be forms with $F,G$ and $F,H$ without common components. If either (1) $V(F)\cap V(G)$ consists of $\deg F\cdot\deg G$ distinct points and $H$ vanishes at all of them, or (2) every point of $V(F)\cap V(G)$ is a simple point of $F$ and $H\bullet F\ge G\bullet F$, then there is a form $B$ of degree $\deg H-\deg G\ge0$ (possibly a nonzero constant), without common component with $F$, such that $B\bullet F=H\bullet F-G\bullet F$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.5, Proposition 1 and Corollary, pp. 61–62], [@Fulton2008, §5.5, Problem 5.29].
>
> Proof routes: (2): $I(P,H\cap F)\ge I(P,G\cap F)$ means $\operatorname{ord}^F_P(H)\ge\operatorname{ord}^F_P(G)$, so $H_*\in(G_*)$ in the discrete valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*)$, and $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*,G_*)\cong(\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*))/(G_*)$. (3): the lemma of §3.3 used for property (5) shows $\mathfrak{m}_P^t\subseteq(F_*,G_*)$ for $t\ge m_P(F)+m_P(G)-1$ when there is no common tangent. (1) is a special case of both. Corollary: Noether's theorem gives $H=AF+BG$, hence $H\bullet F=(BG)\bullet F=B\bullet F+G\bullet F$; a common component of $B$ and $F$ would divide $H$.
>
> Formalization notes: In (2) the book cites Problem 2.44, which is stated for varieties; for reducible $F$ use that $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*)$ is a discrete valuation ring whenever $P$ is a simple point of $F$ (only one component of $F$ passes through $P$, with multiplicity one). The Corollary's hypotheses "$F,H$ without common component" and "$B$ may be constant" are implicit in the book. Depends on: Max Noether's fundamental theorem (including its intersection cycles); Intersection number of projective plane curves at a point; Local ring of an irreducible projective plane curve as a quotient and in affine charts (for (2)); the lemma in the proof of property (5) on [local properties](01-local-properties.md).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti **related**: `TauCeti.ord_eq_length_quotient_span_pair` (TauCeti/RingTheory/Intersection.lean): the order of vanishing of one equation on the other curve is the length of the quotient by both, the comparison used in (2).

### §5.6 Applications of Noether's theorem

> [!milestone] Residual points of a conic on a cubic are collinear
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $C,C'$ cubics without common component with $C'\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_9$ (points with repetition), and $Q$ a conic without common component with $C$ such that $Q\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_6$, where $P_1,\dots,P_6$ are simple points of $C$. Then there is a line $L$, not a component of $C$, with $L\bullet C=P_7+P_8+P_9$; in particular $P_7,P_8,P_9$ are collinear. ($C$ need not be irreducible.)
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Proposition 2, p. 62].
>
> Proof routes: The Corollary to Proposition 1, case (2), with $F=C$, $G=Q$, $H=C'$.
>
> Formalization notes: Depends on: Local criteria for Noether's conditions and the residual intersection corollary.
>

> [!milestone] Pascal's theorem for a hexagon inscribed in an irreducible conic
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $Q$ an irreducible conic and $P_1,\dots,P_6$ distinct points of $Q$. Let $L_i$ be the line through $P_i$ and $P_{i+1}$ (indices mod $6$). Then the three points $L_1\cap L_4$, $L_2\cap L_5$, $L_3\cap L_6$ are collinear. (Opposite sides are distinct lines, so each meets in exactly one point: an irreducible conic contains no three collinear points, by Bézout.)
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Corollary 1 (Pascal), p. 62].
>
> Proof routes: Proposition 2 with $C=L_1L_3L_5$, $C'=L_2L_4L_6$: $C\bullet C'$ is the six vertices plus the three opposite-side points, $Q\bullet C$ is the six vertices, and each vertex is a simple point of $C$ because no three vertices are collinear. Alternatively, Mathlib PR #44986 proves a determinant identity (the $3\times3$ determinant of the three intersection points equals the $6\times6$ determinant of the quadratic Veronese images of the vertices), giving Pascal over any field for any nonzero quadratic form, degenerate conics included, provided the three intersection representatives (iterated cross products) are nonzero.
>
> Formalization notes: Derive it from Proposition 2 as the book does; this exercises Noether's theorem and costs little. If PR #44986 is merged, add a short lemma relating its statement (a `QuadraticForm K (Fin 3 → K)`, `Projectivization.IsCollinear`) to this one (a form of degree $2$ in `MvPolynomial (Fin 3) k`) rather than maintaining two developments; its version is more general (any field, degenerate conics) and would then supersede this milestone's statement. Problem 5.31 (vertices coinciding, sides becoming tangents) and Problem 5.32 (the converse: if the opposite sides of a hexagon meet in collinear points, its vertices lie on a conic) are natural companions. Depends on: Residual points of a conic on a cubic are collinear; Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves.

> [!milestone] Pappus's theorem
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $L_1\ne L_2$ lines, $P_1,P_2,P_3$ distinct points of $L_1$ and $Q_1,Q_2,Q_3$ distinct points of $L_2$, none of them equal to $L_1\cap L_2$. Let $L_{ij}$ be the line through $P_i$ and $Q_j$. For $\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}$ the lines $L_{ij}$ and $L_{ji}$ are distinct; let $R_k=L_{ij}\cap L_{ji}$. Then $R_1,R_2,R_3$ are collinear.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Corollary 2 (Pappus), pp. 62–63].
>
> Proof routes: Proposition 2 with the reducible conic $L_1L_2$ and the hexagon $P_1Q_2P_3Q_1P_2Q_3$: $C=L_{12}L_{31}L_{23}$, $C'=L_{32}L_{21}L_{13}$. Each vertex is a simple point of $C$ and $C$, $C'$ have no common component, by the hypotheses. Pappus is also the degenerate-conic case of the determinant form of Pascal in Mathlib PR #44986.
>
> Formalization notes: The book does not state that the $P_i$, or the $Q_j$, are distinct; it only excludes $L_1\cap L_2$, which already forces $L_1\ne L_2$. Some distinctness is needed for $R_k$ to be defined ($P_1=P_2$ and $Q_1=Q_2$ give $L_{12}=L_{21}$) and for the proof (the vertices must be simple points of $C$); the hypotheses above give both. (If only $P_1=P_2$, the $R_k$ are still defined and collinear, on the line $P_1Q_3$.) Depends on: Residual points of a conic on a cubic are collinear.

> [!milestone] Eight simple points of an irreducible cubic determine the ninth
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $C$ an irreducible cubic and $C',C''$ cubics that are not multiples of $C$. Suppose $C'\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_9$ where the $P_i$ are simple points of $C$, not necessarily distinct, and $C''\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_8+Q$. Then $Q=P_9$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Proposition 3, p. 63].
>
> Proof routes: Suppose $Q\ne P_9$. Take a line $L$ through $P_9$ not through $Q$ and not through the singular point of $C$ (an irreducible cubic has at most one), with $L\bullet C=P_9+R+S$. Then $LC''\bullet C=C'\bullet C+Q+R+S$, and the Corollary to Proposition 1, case (2), with $F=C$, $G=C'$, $H=LC''$, gives a line $L'$ with $L'\bullet C=Q+R+S$. If $R\ne S$ the lines $L,L'$ share two points; if $R=S$ both are the tangent at the simple point $R$; so $L'=L$ and $P_9=Q$.
>
> Formalization notes: The book does not require $L$ to avoid the singular point, which its proof needs (see "Notes on the source"). The classical Cayley–Bacharach statement (if two cubics without common component meet in nine distinct points, every cubic through eight of them passes through the ninth) follows from this one when one of the two cubics is irreducible: a cubic $D$ through eight of the points either is a multiple of $C$ or satisfies $D\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_8+Q$. The general Cayley–Bacharach theorem (no irreducibility) is not in the book. Depends on: Local criteria for Noether's conditions and the residual intersection corollary; Bound on the multiple points of an irreducible plane curve (at most one singular point); Singular points and tangent lines via partial derivatives (uniqueness of the tangent at a simple point).

> [!milestone] Group law on a nonsingular plane cubic
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $C$ a nonsingular cubic (irreducible by Problem 5.21). (a) For $P,Q\in C$ there is a unique line $L$ with $L\bullet C=P+Q+R$ for some $R$ (the line $PQ$ if $P\ne Q$, the tangent at $P$ if $P=Q$); put $\varphi(P,Q)=R$. Then $\varphi$ is symmetric and $\varphi(P,\varphi(P,Q))=Q$. (b) (Proposition 4) For $O\in C$, $P\oplus Q=\varphi(O,\varphi(P,Q))$ makes $C$ an abelian group with identity $O$; $-P=\varphi(P,\varphi(O,O))$, and $L\bullet C=P+Q+R$ for a line $L$ iff $P\oplus Q\oplus R=\varphi(O,O)$. (c) (Problem 5.36, which the book states for the simple points of any irreducible cubic) For another base point $O'$, $P\mapsto\varphi(\varphi(O,O'),P)$ is a group isomorphism $(C,O,\oplus)\to(C,O',\oplus')$. (d) (Problem 5.35) The same construction makes the set $C^\circ$ of simple points of an irreducible cubic $C$ an abelian group, for any $O\in C^\circ$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Addition on a cubic and Proposition 4, pp. 63–64], [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Problems 5.35, 5.36].
>
> Proof routes: (a) Bézout for $L$ and $C$ ($L$ is not a component), with uniqueness of the tangent at a simple point. (b) Commutativity and identity are immediate; associativity: with $L_1\bullet C=P+Q+S'$, $M_1\bullet C=O+S'+S$, $L_2\bullet C=S+R+T'$, $M_2\bullet C=Q+R+U'$, $L_3\bullet C=O+U'+U$, $M_3\bullet C=P+U+T''$, Proposition 3 applied to $C'=L_1L_2L_3$ and $C''=M_1M_2M_3$ gives $T'=T''$, hence $(P\oplus Q)\oplus R=P\oplus(Q\oplus R)$. Coincidences among the points need no separate treatment because Proposition 3 is stated with multiplicities. (d) The third point of a line through two simple points of an irreducible cubic is simple (otherwise the line would meet it with multiplicity $\ge4$).
>
> Formalization notes: Prove Proposition 4 as the book does. It works in every characteristic, for every base point, and needs no flex; it is the main application of Noether's theorem, and the objects (points of an arbitrary cubic in `ℙ k (Fin 3 → k)`, an arbitrary base point) are not Mathlib's. Keep the group structure as a definition depending on $O$ (for example on a type synonym carrying $O$) rather than a global instance. Do not re-derive the structure theory of the group (torsion, explicit formulas, rational points): obtain it from Mathlib through the next milestone, which applies to every nonsingular cubic with a flex, hence to every nonsingular cubic when $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$ (see there). Problem 5.35 as printed also claims the result for reducible cubics without multiple components; that part needs a convention for two points on a line component (see "Notes on the source"), so restrict (d) to irreducible cubics or add the convention. Depends on: Eight simple points of an irreducible cubic determine the ninth; Bézout's theorem for projective plane curves; Nonsingular projective plane curves are irreducible.

> [!milestone] Chord–tangent addition on a Weierstrass cubic agrees with Mathlib's group law
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $W$ a Weierstrass curve over $k$ in Mathlib's sense and $F=$`W.polynomial`, a cubic form. Then $F$ is irreducible, $O=[0:1:0]$ is a simple point and a flex of $F$ with tangent $Z=0$, $F$ is nonsingular iff $\Delta(W)\ne0$, and the simple points of $F$ are exactly the images of Mathlib's nonsingular points (the point at infinity going to $O$). On simple points, the book's $\varphi$ is $\varphi(P,Q)=-(P+Q)$ and the book's addition with base point $O$ is Mathlib's: $P\oplus Q=P+Q$. In particular (Problem 5.38) for $Y^2Z=X^3+aX^2Z+bXZ^2+cZ^3$ ($\operatorname{char}k\ne2$) and $P_i=[x_i:y_i:1]$ with $P_1\oplus P_2=P_3$: if $x_1\ne x_2$ put $\lambda=(y_1-y_2)/(x_1-x_2)$, if $P_1=P_2$ and $y_1\ne0$ put $\lambda=(3x_1^2+2ax_1+b)/(2y_1)$, and $\mu=y_1-\lambda x_1$; then $x_3=\lambda^2-a-x_1-x_2$ and $y_3=-\lambda x_3-\mu$. And (Problem 5.40) if $W$ is defined over a subfield $k_0$ and $O$ is used as base point, the $k_0$-rational points form a subgroup, and $\varphi$ of two $k_0$-rational points is $k_0$-rational.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Problems 5.38, 5.40, pp. 64–65].
>
> Proof routes: Irreducibility: a reducible cubic has a linear factor $\ell$, and $\ell(X,Y,0)$ would divide $F(X,Y,0)=-X^3$, so $\ell$ is $Z$ or $X+cZ$ up to a scalar; but $Z\nmid F$ because $F(X,Y,0)\ne0$, and $X+cZ\nmid F$ because $F(-cZ,Y,Z)$ contains the monomial $Y^2Z$. Intersections with lines: a non-vertical line $Y=\ell X+\nu Z$ cuts $F$ in the cycle read off from the factorization of $W(X,\ell X+\nu)$ (restriction of a curve to a line, Problem 3.21), which is exactly the form of Tau Ceti's `some_add_some_add_some_eq_zero_iff`; a vertical line $X=xZ$ meets $F$ at $(x,y)$, $(x,-y-a_1x-a_3)$ and $O$; the line $Z=0$ meets $F$ at $3O$. These identify $\varphi$ with $-(P+Q)$, and then $P\oplus Q=\varphi(O,-(P+Q))=P+Q$. The formulas of Problem 5.38 are Mathlib's `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.slope`, `addX`, `addY` with $a_1=a_3=0$.
>
> Formalization notes: This is the bridge the library should build instead of a second group law on Weierstrass curves: an `AddEquiv` between the book's group $(C,O,\oplus)$ for $C=V(F)$ and `W.toAffine.Point` (or `W.Point` in projective coordinates, through Mathlib's `WeierstrassCurve.Projective.Point.toAffineAddEquiv`). Combined with the Weierstrass normal form of a cubic with a flex (part (c) of the normal-forms milestone, every characteristic) and with change of base point (Problem 5.36), it identifies the group of a nonsingular cubic **that has a flex**, with any base point, with an elliptic curve in Mathlib. When $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$ every nonsingular cubic has a flex (Corollary (1) of the Hessian milestone, which needs $\operatorname{char}k\nmid4$ for cubics), so there the identification covers all nonsingular cubics. In characteristic $2$ the Hessian vanishes identically and no milestone of the atlas shows that a nonsingular cubic has a flex; the bridge for arbitrary nonsingular cubics in characteristic $2$ is an open extension. Through the bridge, torsion (Problem 5.37), explicit computation (Problems 5.38, 5.39) and rational points (Problem 5.40, via Mathlib's injective base-change map) come from Mathlib and Tau Ceti. The statement can be made over any field for $k$-rational points, matching Mathlib. The book states Problem 5.40 for any nonsingular cubic defined over $k_0$ and any base point $O\in C(k_0)$, which need not be a flex. The bridge covers only Weierstrass cubics, so prove the book's form directly: the line through two $k_0$-rational points, or the tangent at one, is defined over $k_0$; its restriction to $C$ is a binary cubic over $k_0$ with two roots in $k_0$, so the third root is in $k_0$ too (Problem 3.21). Depends on: Group law on a nonsingular plane cubic; Projective normal forms of irreducible conics and cubics (Weierstrass form); Problem 3.21 on [local properties](01-local-properties.md).

> [!milestone] Flexes of a nonsingular cubic and the torsion of its group
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $C$ a nonsingular cubic and $O\in C$ a flex used as base point. (a) $P$ is a flex iff $P\oplus P\oplus P=O$ (Problem 5.37 (b); every characteristic). (b) A line through two distinct flexes meets $C$ in a third flex (Problem 5.34). (c) The flexes form a subgroup; if $\operatorname{char}k\ne3$ it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/3\times\mathbb{Z}/3$, so $C$ has exactly nine flexes (Problem 5.37 (a)). (d) A point $P\ne O$ has order two iff the tangent at $P$ passes through $O$ (Problem 5.37 (c)); if $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$ the points of order dividing two form a group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$ (Problem 5.37 (e)); for $Y^2Z=X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z)$, $\lambda\ne0,1$, $O=[0:1:0]$ and $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$ (in characteristic $2$ this curve is singular), the points of order two are $[0:0:1]$, $[1:0:1]$, $[\lambda:0:1]$ (Problem 5.37 (d)). (e) If $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$, the number of lines through $P\in C$ tangent to $C$ at some point $Q\ne P$ is $4$ if $P$ is not a flex and $3$ if it is (Problem 5.37 (f)).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Problems 5.34, 5.37, p. 64].
>
> Proof routes: With $O$ a flex, $\varphi(O,O)=O$, so collinear triples are those with $P\oplus Q\oplus R=O$; (a), (b), (d) follow directly. (c), (e) and the $2$-torsion count: through the bridge to a Weierstrass curve, Tau Ceti's structure theorem for $N$-torsion over a separably closed field with $N$ invertible ($N=3$, $N=2$); (e) counts solutions of $2Q=-P$ and removes $Q=P$ when $P$ is a flex.
>
> Formalization notes: The book states Problem 5.37 without a characteristic hypothesis (in a book that assumes characteristic $0$ "whenever convenient"); (c) is false in characteristic $3$ (one or three flexes), (d)'s group statement and (e) are false in characteristic $2$, and the curve of the book's 5.37(d) is singular in characteristic $2$. Depends on: Group law on a nonsingular plane cubic; Chord–tangent addition on a Weierstrass cubic agrees with Mathlib's group law; Projective normal forms of irreducible conics and cubics (Weierstrass form).
>

> [!milestone] Points of a nonsingular cubic cut out by a curve of degree m
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed, $C$ a nonsingular cubic. (a) (Problem 5.41) If $O$ is a flex used as base point and $P_1,\dots,P_{3m}\in C$ ($m\ge1$, repetitions allowed), then $P_1\oplus\dots\oplus P_{3m}=O$ iff there is a form $F$ of degree $m$, not divisible by $C$, with $F\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_{3m}$. (b) (Problem 5.42) If $F,F'$ are forms of degree $m$ not divisible by $C$ with $F\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_{3m}$ and $F'\bullet C=P_1+\dots+P_{3m-1}+Q$, then $P_{3m}=Q$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §5.6, Problems 5.41, 5.42, p. 65].
>
> Proof routes: (a) by induction on $m$ with Noether's theorem, following the hint (lines through $P_1,P_2$ and $P_3,P_4$, then the line through the two residual points). (b) as in Proposition 3, from the Corollary to Proposition 1. Alternative for (a): through the Weierstrass bridge, $F\bullet C-3m\,O$ is the divisor of $F/Z^m$, and a degree-zero divisor is principal iff its sum is $O$ (Tau Ceti), together with the fact that every function in $L(3mO)$ is $G/Z^m$ for a form $G$ of degree $m$ (a Noether/Riemann–Roch statement of Chapter 8).
>
> Formalization notes: This is Abel's theorem for plane cubics. Chapter 8 returns to the addition on a cubic in the language of linear equivalence (Problem 8.3), though no later result cites Problems 5.41 or 5.42 by number. Depends on: Group law on a nonsingular plane cubic; Local criteria for Noether's conditions and the residual intersection corollary; Flexes of a nonsingular cubic and the torsion of its group.

## Notes on the source

1. **Numbering and pages.** Theorems and propositions are numbered through the chapter (Theorem 1 in §5.2, Theorem 2 in §5.4, Propositions 1–4 in §§5.5–5.6); Bézout's theorem, Max Noether's fundamental theorem, the Lemma of §5.2 and the Corollary of §5.5 are unnumbered. Problem 5.23 contains its own unnumbered "Theorem" and "Corollary". Page 66 is blank.
2. **Starred problems.** Starred on the printed pages (checked against page images): 5.1, 5.4, 5.5, 5.6, 5.7, 5.8, 5.14, 5.15, 5.16, 5.21, 5.22, 5.26. In the text extraction the star of 5.4 is displaced to the line above the problem number; on the printed p. 54 it is starred like its neighbours.
3. **Characteristic.** The body of the chapter is characteristic-free. Problems 5.23, 5.24, 5.26 say "char$(k)=0$"; Problem 5.28 is in characteristic $p$. Tacit hypotheses: 5.10 divides by $3$ (step (ii)); 5.24 divides by $2$; 5.37 (a) needs $\operatorname{char}k\ne3$, (e), (f) need $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$, and the curve of (d) is nonsingular only if $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$; 5.38's curve is nonsingular only if $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$; 5.39 uses specific integers. In characteristic $3$ the cuspidal cubic $Y^2Z=X^3+X^2Y$ is not projectively equivalent to $Y^2Z=X^3$: all tangent lines of the latter pass through $[1:0:0]$ (since $F_X\equiv0$), while for the former the tangent at $[x:y:1]$ is $xyX+(2y-x^2)Y+y^2Z=0$, and these have no common point (checked by expanding in the basis $x^iy^j$, $j\le1$, of the coordinate ring); so Problem 5.10 fails in characteristic $3$.
4. **Hessian (Problem 5.23).** The book says "Problems 5.22 and 6.47 show that $H\ne0$, for $F$ irreducible". That remark is not what the Corollary uses, and there is no gap. Corollary (1) needs neither $H\ne0$ nor $F\nmid H$: if $H=0$ every point is a flex by Theorem (1). Corollary (2) applies Bézout to $F$ and $H$, which needs $F\nmid H$ ($H\ne0$ alone would not do, since for a cubic $\deg H=\deg F=3$). That follows from Theorem (2): if $F\mid H$, every point of the nonsingular cubic would be a flex, necessarily ordinary, with $I(P,H\cap F)=\infty\ne1$. The outline divides only by $(n-1)^2$, $2$ and $6$ (checked here, including the identity behind step (c)), so it proves the Theorem when $\operatorname{char}k\nmid6(n-1)$, and part (1) when $\operatorname{char}k\nmid2(n-1)$. $H$ vanishes identically for every form in characteristic $2$, and for every form of degree $n$ when $\operatorname{char}k\mid n-1$.
5. **Proposition 1 (2).** The statement needs no extra hypothesis: if $F$ and $G$ share the component $F_1$ of $F$ through the simple point $P$, the hypothesis forces $F_1\mid H$, and then $H_*\in(F_*)$. The proof has a gap: it cites Problem 2.44, stated for varieties (irreducible $F$), and $\operatorname{ord}^F_P$, defined for irreducible $F$. For reducible $F$ one needs that $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{P}^2)/(F_*)$ is a discrete valuation ring at a simple point of $F$, which holds because only one component, of multiplicity one, passes through $P$.
6. **Corollary to Proposition 1.** Implicit hypotheses: $F$ and $G$, and $F$ and $H$, have no common component (otherwise $G\bullet F$ or $H\bullet F$ is undefined), and $B$ may be a nonzero constant ("curve $B$" of degree $0$). Given these, $\deg H\ge\deg G$ follows: $B=0$ would give $F\mid H$.
7. **Proposition 3.** The proof chooses a line $L$ through $P_9$ not through $Q$; it must also avoid the singular point of $C$ (if any). Otherwise $L\bullet C=P_9+2N$ for the double point $N$, the line $L'$ with $L'\bullet C=Q+2N$ is the line $QN$, and it differs from $L=P_9N$ because $L$ misses $Q$: "$L'=L$" fails and the argument gives nothing. The fix costs one more excluded line through $P_9$. The proof also silently assumes $Q\ne P_9$ (otherwise there is nothing to prove). The statement itself is correct; $Q$ is automatically simple, since a singular point would occur in $C''\bullet C$ with coefficient at least $2$.
8. **Pappus (Corollary 2).** Distinctness of $P_1,P_2,P_3$ and of $Q_1,Q_2,Q_3$ is not stated; some of it is needed for the $R_k$ to be defined, and all of it for the proof through Proposition 2. $L_1\ne L_2$ follows from the stated hypothesis that no $P_i$ or $Q_j$ lies on $L_1\cap L_2$.
9. **Problem 5.35.** For a reducible cubic without multiple components, two simple points on a line component span that component (and the tangent at a simple point of it is the component itself), so $\varphi$ is not defined by the book's recipe for them; if the base point $O$ lies on a line component, even $\varphi(O,O)$ is undefined. For pairs on different components the third point is simple, as for irreducible cubics. The claim needs a convention for such pairs; for irreducible cubics it is fine.
10. **Proposition 4.** The book proves only associativity; identity, inverses ($-P=\varphi(P,\varphi(O,O))$) and commutativity are left to the reader.
11. **Theorem 1 (2) of §5.2.** The proof reuses the notation $F_*$ for an affine expansion at $P=[0:0:1]$ and relies on Problem 5.14 (lines through a point avoiding finitely many others), which needs $k$ infinite.
12. **Typos.** Corollary 2 of Bézout: "theses points". Problem 5.40: "emphrational" (a stray LaTeX `\emph`).
13. **Later use.** Chapter 6 (Problem 6.29) and Chapter 8 (Problem 8.3) return to the addition on a cubic; Chapter 7 uses Problem 5.26 and Theorem 2; Chapter 8 uses Theorem 1 of §5.2 and Noether's theorem.

## Deferred

- **Computational exercises** (Problems 5.2, 5.3, 5.18, 5.19, 5.20, 5.39): instances, not theory. They make good regression tests once the library can compute intersection numbers and group operations (5.39 is a computation in Mathlib's group law through the Weierstrass bridge).
- **Problems 5.17, 5.31, 5.32 and 5.33**: exercises that apply the theory rather than extend it, not used later. Problem 5.17 follows from the linear-systems milestone and Bézout's theorem; Problems 5.31–5.33 are applications of Proposition 2 and Pascal's theorem (the Pascal milestone mentions 5.31 and 5.32 as companions).
- **Problem 5.28**: a characteristic-$p$ counterexample; worth recording as an example next to the milestone on generic lines through a point, not as a milestone.
- **Problem 5.43**: open-ended ("for which points"); its answer depends on the group structure and is not a standard result of the theory.
- **Normal forms of cubics in characteristics 2 and 3**: the book's lists are for characteristic $0$; the general Weierstrass shape in the milestone on normal forms covers what later chapters and the bridge to Mathlib need.
