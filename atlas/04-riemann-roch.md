# The Riemann–Roch theorem

This page covers Chapter 8 of Fulton's *Algebraic Curves* [@Fulton2008], "Riemann-Roch Theorem", pp. 97–112: §8.1 Divisors (p. 97), §8.2 The vector spaces $L(D)$ (p. 99), §8.3 Riemann's theorem (p. 101), §8.4 Derivations and differentials (p. 104), §8.5 Canonical divisors (p. 106) and §8.6 Riemann–Roch theorem (p. 108), together with the remarks of Appendix A, "Nonzero characteristic" (p. 113), that bear on this chapter.

The chapter mixes two kinds of mathematics. One is the theory of an algebraic function field $K$ of one variable over $k$: divisors, the spaces $L(D)$, the genus, Riemann's theorem, differentials and the Riemann–Roch theorem. Tau Ceti already has all of it, in more generality than the book, following Stichtenoth's book [@Stichtenoth2009] rather than Fulton's proofs. The other is the classical plane-curve side, due to Brill and Noether, which only a plane model can express: the divisor $\operatorname{div}(G)$ cut out by a form $G$, adjoint curves, the residue theorem, the genus formula for a curve with ordinary multiple points, canonical divisors cut out by adjoints of degree $n-3$, and Noether's reduction lemma. Tau Ceti has the nonsingular case of some of these and nothing for singular curves. The milestones below are therefore the plane-curve side, plus the bridges that identify the book's objects with Tau Ceti's; the function-field results appear as milestones only where a bridge has to be built, and otherwise as rows of the correspondence table.

## Conventions

### The book

- **The field.** From Chapter 3 on, $k$ is a fixed algebraically closed field; the preface adds that characteristic zero is assumed "whenever convenient", often without comment in the problems, with Appendix A on arbitrary characteristic. In Chapter 8 the explicit characteristic hypotheses are: Proposition 6(2) and the derivative $df/dt$ defined after it (char 0); Example (2) of §8.3 (char $\neq 2$); Problems 8.36(b),(c), 8.37 and 8.38 (char 0). Characteristic zero is also used silently in the proofs of Proposition 7, the definition of $\operatorname{ord}_P(\omega)$ (both through the derivative $df/dt$), Proposition 8 (which never checks $F_X \neq 0$) and the reduction lemma (the choice of a line), through the existence of good plane models (Chapter 7). The proof of Proposition 6(1) does not need it: an irreducible $F$ over the perfect field $k$ is not in $k[X^p, Y^p]$, so $F_X$ or $F_Y$ is nonzero and the book's "we may assume $F_Y \neq 0$" holds after exchanging $x$ and $y$. Appendix A says how to remove the characteristic hypotheses. The drafting agent's check, result by result, repeated in the fact-check (not the book's claim): every proposition, lemma, theorem and corollary of the chapter holds in every characteristic, with the modifications recorded in each milestone, except Proposition 6(2) as stated (in characteristic $p$, $dx$ is a basis exactly when $K/k(x)$ is separable); Example (2) of §8.3 and Problems 8.36(b), (c), 8.37 and 8.38 carry their own characteristic hypotheses.
- **Curves and models.** $C$ is an irreducible projective curve, $f\colon X \to C$ its nonsingular model (§7.5), $K = k(C) = k(X)$. Points of $X$ are identified with the places of $K$, i.e. with the discrete valuation rings of $K$ containing $k$, and $\operatorname{ord}_P$ is the order function at $P$. A place $P$ is *centered* at $f(P) \in C$. Since $k$ is algebraically closed, every place has residue field $k$.
- **Plane curves** are nonzero forms up to a scalar, possibly reducible and with multiple components. A plane curve $G$ "not containing $C$" means $F \nmid G$, where $F$ is the irreducible form of $C$. For such $G$, $\operatorname{ord}_P(G)$ is the order at $P$ of the dehomogenization of $G$ at a coordinate not vanishing at $f(P)$ (§7.5), and $\operatorname{div}(G) = \sum_P \operatorname{ord}_P(G)\,P$.
- **Divisors** on $X$ are finite formal $\mathbb{Z}$-combinations of points; $\deg$ is the sum of the coefficients; $\equiv$ is linear equivalence. $L(D) = \{f \in K : \operatorname{div}(f) + D \geq 0\} \cup \{0\}$ and $l(D) = \dim_k L(D)$.
- **Adjoints** are defined only when $C$ is a plane curve with only ordinary multiple points: $r_Q = m_{f(Q)}(C)$ for $Q \in X$, $E = \sum_{Q \in X} (r_Q - 1)\,Q$, and $G$ is adjoint when $\operatorname{div}(G) \geq E$. In the proof of Proposition 5, $V_m$ is the **vector space** of adjoint forms of degree $m$ (including $0$ and the multiples of $F$), not the projective space of adjoint curves; Fulton warns about this himself. Theorem 1 of §5.2 counts projective dimension, one less.
- **The genus** is the least $g$ with $l(D) \geq \deg D + 1 - g$ for all $D$; it depends only on $K$.
- **Differentials** are Kähler differentials $\Omega = \Omega_k(K)$, constructed by hand in §8.4. The order $\operatorname{ord}_P(\omega)$ of $\omega \neq 0$ is $\operatorname{ord}_P(f)$ for $\omega = f\,dt$ with $t$ a uniformizing parameter at $P$; a canonical divisor is $\operatorname{div}(\omega)$ for some $\omega \neq 0$. $\Omega(D) = \{\omega : \operatorname{div}(\omega) \geq D\}$ (with $0$), and $\delta(D) = \dim_k \Omega(D)$.
- **Numbering.** In this edition propositions, theorems and lemmas are numbered consecutively through each chapter (Propositions 1–9, Lemmas 1–3 in Chapter 8) and cited with the section, e.g. "Proposition 3 of §7.5"; corollaries are numbered per result; the Residue Theorem, Riemann's Theorem, the Riemann–Roch Theorem and Noether's Reduction Lemma are unnumbered; problems are numbered 8.1–8.38 through the chapter. Starred problems (8.8, 8.11, 8.13) are used freely in the text.

### The libraries

- **Function fields (Tau Ceti).** `TauCeti.IsFunctionField k F` is a `Prop` passed as an explicit hypothesis `hF`, not a typeclass; it allows any field `k`. Exactness of constants is the separate hypothesis `hex : IsIntegrallyClosedIn k F`, automatic over an algebraically closed `k` (`TauCeti.isIntegrallyClosedIn_of_isAlgClosed`, `TauCeti/RingTheory/IntegralClosure/IntegrallyClosed.lean`).
- **Places** `TauCeti.Place k F` are normalized discrete valuations trivial on `k`, in Mathlib's multiplicative convention (values in `ℤᵐ⁰`); `TauCeti.Place.ord : F → ℤ` is the additive order, with junk value `ord 0 = 0`. Over an algebraically closed `k` every place has degree one (`TauCeti.Place.degree_eq_one_of_isAlgClosed_of_isFunctionField`).
- **Divisors** `TauCeti.Divisor k F` are `WeilDivisor (Place k F)`, finitely supported functions to `ℤ`, with the lattice operations `⊔`, `⊓`. `TauCeti.Divisor.degree` weights each place by its residue degree; over an algebraically closed `k` it is the coefficient sum (`TauCeti.Divisor.degree_apply_of_isAlgClosed`). Principal divisors are defined for units only: `TauCeti.Divisor.principal hF (z : Fˣ)`, with `TauCeti.Divisor.zeros` and `TauCeti.Divisor.poles` for $(z)_0$, $(z)_\infty$. Linear equivalence is `(TauCeti.Place.orderSystem hF).LinearlyEquivalent`, and the class group is `(TauCeti.Place.orderSystem hF).ClassGroup`.
- **$L(D)$ and $l(D)$** are `TauCeti.riemannRochSpace D : Submodule k F` and `TauCeti.Divisor.dim D : ℕ`; the genus is `TauCeti.genus k F : ℕ`, defined as the supremum of $\deg D + 1 - l(D)$ truncated to `ℕ`, a junk value when `F` is not a function field or the constants are not exact. Over an algebraically closed `k` it is Fulton's genus.
- **Canonical divisors** in Tau Ceti come from **Weil differentials** (linear forms on repartitions), not from Kähler differentials: `TauCeti.weilDifferentialDivisor`, `TauCeti.weilDifferentialOrder` and `TauCeti.canonicalClass`. The Kähler and Weil differentials are identified by `TauCeti.kaehlerDifferentialEquivWeilDifferentialOfSeparating`, but no divisor of a Kähler differential is defined; this is a bridge the library must state (see the milestone [*Order and divisor of a differential on the nonsingular model*](#84-derivations-and-differentials)).
- **Trap: two "residue theorems".** Tau Ceti's residue theorem (`TauCeti.finsum_repartitionDualComponent_eq_zero`, `TauCeti.finsum_kaehlerResidueOfPerfectField_eq_zero`) is the theorem that the residues of a differential sum to zero. Fulton's Residue Theorem in §8.1 is the Brill–Noether *Restsatz* about adjoint curves. They are unrelated.
- **Plane curves in Tau Ceti** are only nonsingular ones: a homogeneous `G : MvPolynomial (Fin 3) k` of degree `d`, nonsingular at every projective zero over an algebraically closed extension `K` of `k` (the hypothesis `hsm`), read through affine coordinates `x y : F` with `aeval ![x, y, 1] G = 0`, or through the scheme `TauCeti.AlgebraicGeometry.projectivePlaneCurve hG`. Their genus is stated in `ℕ` as `(d - 1) * (d - 2) / 2`. For the singular genus formula use `ℤ` and the doubled form $2g = (n-1)(n-2) - \sum r_P(r_P - 1)$, to avoid truncated subtraction and division.
- **Mathlib** has Kähler differentials `Ω[S⁄R]` with their universal property, derivations, homogeneous polynomials (`MvPolynomial.IsHomogeneous`, `MvPolynomial.homogeneousSubmodule`) and the group law of Weierstrass curves, but no divisors of curves, no genus and no Riemann–Roch theorem.
- **Multiplicities and intersection numbers** $m_P(G)$, $I(P, C \cap G)$ come from [01-local-properties.md](01-local-properties.md) and [02-projective-plane-curves.md](02-projective-plane-curves.md); follow the conventions there (in particular `ℕ` versus `ℕ∞` for intersection numbers). On this page every intersection number is between curves without common components, hence finite.

## Milestones

### §8.1 Divisors

> [!milestone] Divisors cut out by forms on the nonsingular model of a plane curve
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F \in k[X,Y,Z]$ an irreducible form of degree $n \geq 1$, $C = V(F)$, $K = k(C)$, and identify the nonsingular model $X$ with the set of places of $K/k$. Every place $P$ has a unique center $c(P) \in C$, the point $Q$ with $\mathcal{O}_Q(C) \subseteq \mathcal{O}_P$ and $\mathfrak{m}_Q \subseteq \mathfrak{m}_P$. For a form $G$ of degree $m$ with $F \nmid G$, put $\operatorname{ord}_P(G) = \operatorname{ord}_P(G/L^m)$ for any linear form $L$ with $L(c(P)) \neq 0$. Then:
> (a) $\operatorname{ord}_P(G) \geq 0$, it does not depend on $L$, and $\operatorname{ord}_P(G) > 0$ exactly when $G(c(P)) = 0$;
> (b) $\operatorname{div}(G) = \sum_P \operatorname{ord}_P(G)\,P$ is an effective divisor with $\sum_{c(P) = Q} \operatorname{ord}_P(G) = I(Q, C \cap G)$ for every $Q \in C$, hence $\deg \operatorname{div}(G) = mn$;
> (c) $\operatorname{div}(GH) = \operatorname{div}(G) + \operatorname{div}(H)$, and if $\deg G = \deg H$ then $G/H \in K^\times$ and $\operatorname{div}(G/H) = \operatorname{div}(G) - \operatorname{div}(H)$; in particular every $z \in K^\times$ has $\deg \operatorname{div}(z) = 0$ (Proposition 1);
> (d) two divisors satisfy $D \equiv D'$ if and only if there are forms $G, G'$ of the same degree, not divisible by $F$, with $D + \operatorname{div}(G) = D' + \operatorname{div}(G')$ (Proposition 2(5)).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.1, definition of $\operatorname{div}(G)$, Proposition 1 and Proposition 2(5), pp. 97–98], resting on [@Fulton2008, §7.5, Proposition 2].
>
> Proof routes: (a) and (c) are bookkeeping with the definition. (b) is Proposition 2 of §7.5 ($I(Q, C\cap G) = \sum_{c(P)=Q} \operatorname{ord}_P(G)$, proved with the finite-dimensionality of $\Gamma(U')/\Gamma(U)$ from Lemma 2 of §7.5) followed by Bézout's theorem. (d): every $z \in K^\times$ is a quotient of two forms of the same degree, and (c) applies.
>
> Formalization notes: Take the places from Tau Ceti (`TauCeti.Place k K`) and do not build the nonsingular model as a scheme for this chapter; Fulton identifies points of $X$ with places anyway. For $K$, use the function field of `TauCeti.AlgebraicGeometry.projectivePlaneCurve hG` or the fraction field of an affine chart; `TauCeti.AlgebraicGeometry.projectivePlaneCurve.functionFieldEquiv_germToFunctionField_mk` already sends $p/X_i^m$ to the dehomogenized class. A chart-free definition avoids choosing $L$: $\operatorname{ord}_P(G) = \max_i \operatorname{ord}_P(G/X_i^m)$ over the coordinates $X_i$ not divisible by $F$, because the maximum is attained at every coordinate not vanishing at $c(P)$; so $\operatorname{div}(G) = \bigsqcup_i \operatorname{div}(G/X_i^m)$ (supremum in the divisor lattice), which for $G = Z$ is Tau Ceti's $(x)_\infty \sqcup (y)_\infty$. The center $c(P)$ is then $[\,\overline{X_0/X_i} : \overline{X_1/X_i} : \overline{X_2/X_i}\,]$ for such an $i$ (residues in $k$). Carry $F \nmid G$ as a hypothesis: in Lean a form divisible by $F$ is $0$ in $K$ and `Place.ord` returns the junk value $0$. Proposition 1 itself needs no plane model: Tau Ceti proves it for every function field (`TauCeti.Divisor.degree_principal`). The book's proofs use starred Problem 4.17 (finitely many zeros and poles) and, through Proposition 2 of §7.5, starred Problems 2.44, 2.50 and 2.53. Dependencies: the nonsingular model and Proposition 2 of §7.5 on [03-resolution-of-singularities.md](03-resolution-of-singularities.md); Bézout's theorem on [02-projective-plane-curves.md](02-projective-plane-curves.md).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti **partial**: the function-field side is complete (`TauCeti.Divisor.principal` in `TauCeti/FieldTheory/FunctionField/Divisor/Principal.lean`, `TauCeti.Divisor.degree_principal` in `TauCeti/FieldTheory/FunctionField/Divisor/ProductFormula.lean`; linear equivalence via `TauCeti.Divisor.linearlyEquivalent_iff` in `.../Divisor/Principal.lean`); for nonsingular curves, `TauCeti.degree_sup_poles_of_isHomogeneous` (`TauCeti/FieldTheory/FunctionField/PlaneCurve/Projective.lean`) proves that the divisor cut out by the line at infinity has degree $d$. There is no divisor of a general form, no center map, and no identity $\sum \operatorname{ord}_P(G) = I(Q, C\cap G)$; `TauCeti.ord_eq_length_quotient_span_pair` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/Intersection.lean`) is related local algebra.

> [!milestone] Adjoint curves of a plane curve with ordinary multiple points
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic) and $C = V(F)$ an irreducible plane curve of degree $n$ all of whose multiple points are ordinary. For a place $P$ put $r_P = m_{c(P)}(C)$ and $E = \sum_P (r_P - 1)\,P$. Then $E$ is effective, supported on the places centered at multiple points, and $\deg E = \sum_{Q \in C} m_Q(C)\,(m_Q(C) - 1)$. A form $G$ with $F \nmid G$ satisfies $\operatorname{div}(G) \geq E$ (that is, $G$ is an *adjoint* of $C$) if and only if $m_Q(G) \geq m_Q(C) - 1$ for every multiple point $Q$ of $C$. If $C$ is nonsingular, $E = 0$ and every curve is an adjoint.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.1, p. 98], using [@Fulton2008, Problem 7.19].
>
> Proof routes: An ordinary $r$-fold point has exactly $r$ places over it (Step (2) of §7.2), which gives $\deg E$. Starred Problem 7.19 says that for $s \leq r$, $m_Q(G) \geq s$ if and only if $\operatorname{ord}_{P_i}(G) \geq s$ at each of the $r$ places $P_i$ over $Q$; apply it with $s = r - 1$.
>
> Formalization notes: Define the adjoint condition on forms by multiplicities, not by divisors: the set $V_m$ of forms of degree $m$ with $m_Q(G) \geq m_Q(C) - 1$ at every multiple point is a $k$-subspace of `MvPolynomial.homogeneousSubmodule (Fin 3) k m`, contains $0$ and the multiples of $F$, and is what Theorem 1 of §5.2 counts. The theorem above then identifies it with the divisor condition for $F \nmid G$. The natural generalization to arbitrary singularities replaces $E$ by the conductor of $\mathcal{O}_Q(C)$ in its integral closure; Fulton develops that theory in [@Fulton2002Adjoints]. It is not needed for the book. The book's proof is starred Problem 7.19 (itself resting on starred Problem 7.5). Dependencies: "ordinary multiple point" and multiplicities from [01-local-properties.md](01-local-properties.md); Problem 7.19 and the places over an ordinary multiple point on [03-resolution-of-singularities.md](03-resolution-of-singularities.md).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti — (searched for adjoint curves, conductors of plane curves and ordinary multiple points by name and keyword); related API for the generalization: `TauCeti.Algebra.conductor` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/Conductor.lean`) and `AlgebraicGeometry.Scheme.Hom.conductor` (`TauCeti/AlgebraicGeometry/IdealSheaf/Conductor/Basic.lean`), the conductor of an algebra and of a finite morphism, with nothing specific to plane curves.

> [!milestone] Brill–Noether residue theorem for plane curves
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $C = V(F)$ an irreducible plane curve with only ordinary multiple points, and $E$ as in the previous milestone. Let $D, D'$ be effective divisors on $X$ with $D' \equiv D$, and let $G$ be an adjoint of degree $m$ with $\operatorname{div}(G) = D + E + A$ for some effective $A$. Then there is an adjoint $G'$ of degree $m$, with $F \nmid G'$, such that $\operatorname{div}(G') = D' + E + A$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.1, Residue Theorem, p. 98].
>
> Proof routes: The book's: choose forms $H, H'$ of equal degree with $D + \operatorname{div}(H) = D' + \operatorname{div}(H')$ (Proposition 2(5)). Then $\operatorname{div}(GH) \geq \operatorname{div}(H') + E$, so Noether's conditions hold at every point of $C$ for $F, H', GH$ (Proposition 3 of §7.5 at the multiple points, the simple-point criterion elsewhere), and Max Noether's theorem gives $GH = F'F + G'H'$ with $\deg G' = m$; then $\operatorname{div}(G') = \operatorname{div}(GH) - \operatorname{div}(H') = D' + E + A$. The book does not check $F \nmid G'$; it holds because $F \nmid GH$.
>
> Formalization notes: Isolate the step used twice in the chapter (here, and in the surjectivity step of the proof of Proposition 5, which also gives its Corollary 3) as one lemma, an *adjoint division lemma*: if $R, S$ are forms with $F \nmid S$ and $\operatorname{div}(R) \geq \operatorname{div}(S) + E$ (or $F \mid R$), then $R = AS + BF$ for forms $A, B$ with $\deg A = \deg R - \deg S$. It is Max Noether's fundamental theorem plus the local criteria for Noether's conditions. Dependencies: Proposition 2(5) (previous milestones), Max Noether's fundamental theorem ([02-projective-plane-curves.md](02-projective-plane-curves.md)), Proposition 3 of §7.5 ([03-resolution-of-singularities.md](03-resolution-of-singularities.md)).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti — (Tau Ceti's "residue theorem" is the unrelated sum-of-residues theorem, see Conventions).

> [!milestone] Chord–tangent group of a nonsingular cubic as its divisor class group
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $C$ a nonsingular plane cubic, $P_0 \in C$, and $\oplus$ the chord–tangent addition on $C$ with origin $P_0$ (§5.6). Then: (a) for $P, Q \in C$, $P \equiv Q$ if and only if $P = Q$; (b) for $P, Q, R, S \in C$, let $T$ and $T'$ be the third points of intersection of $C$ with the line through $P, Q$ and with the line through $R, S$ (the tangent line when the two points coincide); then $P + Q \equiv R + S$ if and only if $T = T'$ (corrected from the book's wording, see Notes on the source); (c) $P \oplus Q = R$ if and only if $P + Q \equiv R + P_0$; (d) $P \mapsto [P - P_0]$ is a group isomorphism from $(C, \oplus)$ onto the group $C^0(X)$ of degree-zero divisor classes. In particular (c), together with the bijectivity of $P \mapsto [P - P_0]$, gives a second proof of Proposition 4 of §5.6, that $\oplus$ makes $C$ an abelian group.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, Problems 8.3 and 8.6, p. 99].
>
> Proof routes: The book's hint: lines are adjoints of degree 1 of a nonsingular cubic, so the residue theorem moves divisors along lines. For (a) one can also argue that $P \equiv Q$ with $P \neq Q$ would make $C$ rational (Corollary to Proposition 4), against genus one. (b): $\operatorname{div}(L_{PQ}/L_{RS}) = P + Q + T - R - S - T'$, so $P + Q \equiv R + S$ iff $T \equiv T'$ iff $T = T'$ by (a). Surjectivity in (d) is Riemann–Roch in genus one: every degree-one divisor class contains exactly one point. Alternatively, transport Tau Ceti's function-field statement: in genus one, $P \mapsto [P - P_0]$ is a bijection from the rational places onto $C^0$, and the group structure it induces satisfies $P \oplus Q = R \iff P + Q \sim R + P_0$; (c) identifies this structure with the chord–tangent one, so only the plane-geometric part (b) needs the plane model.
>
> Formalization notes: Aim for any nonsingular cubic and any base point; the Weierstrass case with $P_0$ the flex at infinity is already done (below). Reducing a general cubic to Weierstrass form by a projective change of coordinates is Problem 5.24 in the book (characteristic 0); in other characteristics use Tau Ceti's genus-one Weierstrass coordinates. Dependencies: residue theorem; the group law of §5.6 on [02-projective-plane-curves.md](02-projective-plane-curves.md); genus of a nonsingular cubic (milestone *Genus formula for plane curves with only ordinary multiple points*).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: the chord–tangent group `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.Point` and its injection `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.Point.toClass`, `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.Point.toClass_injective` into the ideal class group of the coordinate ring (`Mathlib/AlgebraicGeometry/EllipticCurve/Affine/Point.lean`). Tau Ceti **partial**: for Weierstrass curves with origin at infinity, `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.pointEquivDegreeZeroDivisorClass` (`TauCeti/AlgebraicGeometry/EllipticCurve/Affine/FunctionField/Divisor/Class.lean`) is the group isomorphism of (d), and `WeierstrassCurve.Affine.divisorSum_eq_zero_iff` (`.../Divisor/Sum.lean`) characterizes principal divisors; for function fields of genus one, `TauCeti.Place.degreeOneEquivDegreeZeroClassGroup` and `TauCeti.Place.degreeOneAddCommGroup_add_eq_iff` (`TauCeti/FieldTheory/FunctionField/Elliptic/Basic.lean`), and `TauCeti.Place.eq_of_linearlyEquivalent_ofPoint_of_genus_pos` (`.../Consequences/PointSeparation.lean`) for (a). Nothing for a general cubic or base point, or for (b).

### §8.3 Riemann's theorem

> [!milestone] Riemann's theorem on the nonsingular model of a plane curve
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $C$ an irreducible projective curve (in particular a plane curve), $X$ its nonsingular model and $K = k(C)$. There is an integer $g \geq 0$, the genus of $C$, least with $l(D) \geq \deg D + 1 - g$ for every divisor $D$ on $X$. Moreover: (Corollary 1) if $l(D_0) = \deg D_0 + 1 - g$ and $D \equiv D' \geq D_0$, then $l(D) = \deg D + 1 - g$; (Corollary 2) for $x \in K \setminus k$, $g = \deg(r(x)_0) - l(r(x)_0) + 1$ for all large $r$; (Corollary 3) there is $N$ with $l(D) = \deg D + 1 - g$ whenever $\deg D > N$. The genus depends only on $K$: birationally equivalent curves have the same genus.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.3, Riemann's Theorem and Corollaries 1–3, pp. 101–102], with [@Fulton2008, §8.2, Propositions 3 and 4].
>

> [!milestone] Adjoints of degree m compute the Riemann–Roch spaces L(E_m)
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $C = V(F)$ an irreducible plane curve of degree $n$ with only ordinary multiple points, $E$ as above, $L$ a linear form with $F \nmid L$, and for $m \geq 0$ let $E_m = m\operatorname{div}(L) - E$, $V_m$ the vector space of adjoint forms of degree $m$ (forms with $m_Q(G) \geq m_Q(C) - 1$ at every multiple point, including $0$ and the multiples of $F$), and $W_{m-n}$ the space of all forms of degree $m - n$ ($0$ if $m < n$). Then
> $$0 \longrightarrow W_{m-n} \xrightarrow{\;H \,\mapsto\, FH\;} V_m \xrightarrow{\;G \,\mapsto\, G/L^m\;} L(E_m) \longrightarrow 0$$
> is exact. Hence every $h \in L(E_m)$ is $H/L^m$ for an adjoint $H$ of degree $m$, $V_m \cong L(E_m)$ when $m < n$, and $l(E_m) = \dim V_m - \dim W_{m-n}$. Furthermore $\dim V_m \geq \frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2} - \sum_{Q \in C} \frac{r_Q(r_Q-1)}{2}$, with equality once $m \geq \sum_Q (r_Q - 1) - 1$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.3, proof of Proposition 5 and Corollary 3(a), pp. 103–104]; the dimension count is [@Fulton2008, §5.2, Theorem 1].
>
> Proof routes: $G \mapsto G/L^m$ lands in $L(E_m)$ because $\operatorname{div}(G/L^m) + E_m = \operatorname{div}(G) - E \geq 0$, and its kernel is the multiples of $F$. Surjectivity: write $f \in L(E_m)$ as $R/S$ with forms of equal degree; then $\operatorname{div}(RL^m) \geq \operatorname{div}(S) + E$, so the adjoint division lemma gives $RL^m = AS + BF$, and $f = A/L^m$ with $A \in V_m$. The dimension bound is Theorem 1 of §5.2 applied to the conditions $m_Q \geq r_Q - 1$, plus one for passing from projective to vector-space dimension.

> Formalization notes: The book takes $L = Z$ meeting $C$ in $n$ distinct points; the exact sequence needs only $F \nmid L$, and allowing any such $L$ removes a characteristic-$p$ detour (Appendix A). State the sequence with `MvPolynomial.homogeneousSubmodule` and `TauCeti.riemannRochSpace`. Watch the off-by-one between Theorem 1 of §5.2 (projective dimension) and $\dim V_m$. The book's proof uses the adjoint criterion, i.e. starred Problem 7.19. > [!milestone] Genus formula for plane curves with only ordinary multiple points
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic) and $C$ an irreducible plane curve of degree $n \geq 1$ whose multiple points are all ordinary, with $r_Q = m_Q(C)$. Then
> $$g = \frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2} - \sum_{Q \in C} \frac{r_Q(r_Q - 1)}{2},$$
> the sum running over all points (simple points contribute $0$). Moreover $\deg E_{n-3} = 2g - 2$ and $l(E_{n-3}) \geq g$, with $E_{n-3}$ as in the previous milestone. In particular a nonsingular plane curve of degree $n$ has genus $(n-1)(n-2)/2$: lines and conics have genus $0$, nonsingular cubics genus $1$, nonsingular quartics genus $3$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.3, Proposition 5, Corollary 3(b) and the Examples after it, pp. 102–104].
>
> Proof routes: The book's: for large $m$, the exact sequence of the previous milestone and Theorem 1(2) of §5.2 give $l(E_m) = \deg E_m + 1 - g^*$ with $g^*$ the right-hand side, while $\deg E_m = mn - \sum r_Q(r_Q - 1)$ grows with $m$, so Corollary 3 of Riemann's theorem gives $g = g^*$. Corollary 3(b) is the same sequence at $m = n - 3$ with the lower bound of Theorem 1(1). >
> Formalization notes: Prefer the book's route: the adjoint machinery it needs is required anyway by the canonical-divisor, reduction-lemma and linear-system milestones. > [!milestone] Genus bound by the virtual genus of a plane curve

### §8.4 Derivations and differentials

> [!milestone] Order and divisor of a differential on the nonsingular model
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed and $K$ a function field of one variable over $k$, with places $X$.
> (a) $\Omega_k(K)$ is a one-dimensional $K$-vector space; for $x \in K$, $dx$ is a basis exactly when $K/k(x)$ is finite and separable (in characteristic $0$: exactly when $x \notin k$).
> (b) Every uniformizing parameter $t$ at a place $P$ is separating, and $f \in \mathcal{O}_P(X)$ implies $df/dt \in \mathcal{O}_P(X)$.
> (c) For $\omega \neq 0$, $\operatorname{ord}_P(\omega) := \operatorname{ord}_P(\omega/dt)$, $t$ a uniformizer at $P$, does not depend on $t$ and vanishes for all but finitely many $P$; $\operatorname{div}(\omega) = \sum_P \operatorname{ord}_P(\omega) P$ satisfies $\operatorname{div}(f\omega) = \operatorname{div}(f) + \operatorname{div}(\omega)$, and the canonical divisors $\operatorname{div}(\omega)$ form exactly one linear-equivalence class.
> (d) Bridge: for $\omega \neq 0$, $\operatorname{div}(\omega)$ equals Tau Ceti's divisor `weilDifferentialDivisor` of the Weil differential that corresponds to $\omega$ under the Kähler–Weil comparison; hence the book's canonical class is `TauCeti.canonicalClass`.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.4, Lemmas 2 and 3, Propositions 6 and 7, pp. 105–106], [@Fulton2008, §8.5, definition of $\operatorname{ord}_P(\omega)$ and of canonical divisors, pp. 106–107], [@Fulton2008, Appendix A, p. 113]. Part (d) is not in the book.

### §8.5 Canonical divisors

> [!milestone] Canonical divisors of a plane curve are cut out by adjoints of degree n − 3
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $C = V(F)$ an irreducible plane curve of degree $n \geq 3$ with only ordinary multiple points, and $E$ as above.
> (a) For every nonzero form $G$ of degree $n - 3$ (necessarily $F \nmid G$), $\operatorname{div}(G) - E$ is a canonical divisor; for $n = 3$, $-E$ is canonical. Explicitly, in coordinates where $Z$ meets $C$ in $n$ distinct points, $[1:0:0] \notin C$ and no tangent at a multiple point passes through $[1:0:0]$, with $x = X/Z$, $y = Y/Z$ and $f_y = F_Y(x,y,1)$: $\operatorname{div}(dx/f_y) = (n-3)\operatorname{div}(Z) - E$.
> (b) Consequently $\deg W = 2g - 2$ and $l(W) \geq g$ for every canonical $W$; and, with Riemann–Roch, the effective canonical divisors are exactly the $\operatorname{div}(G) - E$ for nonzero adjoints $G$ of degree $n - 3$, the adjoints of degree $n-3$ form a space of dimension $g$, and the holomorphic differentials are exactly the $H(x,y,1)\,dx/f_y$ with $H$ an adjoint of degree $n - 3$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.5, Proposition 8 and its Corollary, p. 107]. The description of effective canonical divisors in (b) combines Proposition 8 with [@Fulton2008, §8.3, Corollary 3(a) to Proposition 5] and [@Fulton2008, §8.6, Corollary 1 to the Riemann–Roch Theorem]; the book does not state it as one result.
>
> Proof routes: The book's: all $\operatorname{div}(G) - E$ with $\deg G = n-3$ are linearly equivalent, so it suffices to prove $\operatorname{div}(dx) - \operatorname{div}(F_Y) = -2\operatorname{div}(Z) - E$ place by place, using $dx = -(F_Y/F_X)\,dy$: at places over $Z \cap C$, $1/y$ is a uniformizer and $F_X \neq 0$ there (starred Problem 5.16); at an affine point where the horizontal line is tangent, the point is simple, $x$ is a uniformizer and $F_Y \neq 0$; elsewhere $y$ is a uniformizer and $\operatorname{ord}(F_X) = r - 1$ at each place over an $r$-fold point (starred Problem 7.4). In Lean the local steps are Tau Ceti lemmas: the order of the Weil differential of a uniformizer is $0$, and $\operatorname{ord}_P(dy/dx) = v_P(dy) - v_P(dx)$. Alternative: $\operatorname{div}(dx) = -2(x)_\infty + \operatorname{Diff}(K/k(x))$ (in Tau Ceti) plus Dedekind's different formula with the conductor $E$ (not in Tau Ceti for singular curves).
>
> Formalization notes: The natural general form, valid for every $n \geq 1$, is: $(n-3)\operatorname{div}(L) - E$ is canonical for every linear form $L$ with $F \nmid L$; for $n = 1, 2$ the curve is nonsingular and this is Tau Ceti's theorem. Characteristic $p$: the coordinate conditions of the book suffice. If $Z$ meets $C$ in $n \geq 2$ distinct points, then $F_X \neq 0$ and $F_Y \neq 0$ as polynomials (if, say, $F \in k[X, Y^p, Z]$, then $F(1, Y, 0)$ is a $p$-th power, so every point of $Z \cap C$ other than $[0:1:0]$ has intersection multiplicity divisible by $p$), so $x$ and $y$ are separating and $dx \neq 0$; such a line exists by Appendix A, and $[1:0:0]$ is then chosen on it off $C$ and off the finitely many tangents at multiple points. Starred Problems 5.16 and 7.4 are used; both hold in every characteristic (7.4 because the tangents at an ordinary point are distinct). Dependencies: previous milestones (adjoints, $L(E_m)$, genus formula); order and divisor of a differential.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti **partial**: nonsingular curves only. `TauCeti.divisorClass_sub_three_zsmul_sup_poles_eq_canonicalClass_of_isHomogeneous` and `TauCeti.riemannRochSpace_sub_three_zsmul_sup_poles_eq_span_of_isHomogeneous` (`TauCeti/FieldTheory/FunctionField/PlaneCurve/Projective.lean`) prove (a) and (b) with $E = 0$; in an affine chart, `TauCeti.weilDifferentialDivisor_repartitionDualMul_inv_weilDifferentialCotrace_of_aeval_eq_zero` ($\operatorname{div}(dx/f_y) = (n-3)(x)_\infty$) and `TauCeti.weilDifferentialFiltration_zero_eq_span_of_aeval_eq_zero` (the holomorphic differentials) in `.../PlaneCurve/RegularDifferential.lean`. ### §8.6 Riemann–Roch theorem

> [!milestone] Noether's reduction lemma
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $X$ the nonsingular model of a curve, $W$ a canonical divisor, $D$ a divisor with $l(D) > 0$ and $P \in X$. If $l(W - D - P) \neq l(W - D)$, then $l(D + P) = l(D)$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.6, Noether's Reduction Lemma, p. 108].
>
> Proof routes: The book's (Brill–Noether): choose a plane model $C$ with only ordinary multiple points on which $P$ is simple (starred Problem 7.21(a)) and a line $Z$ missing $P$; reduce to $W = E_{n-3}$ and $D \geq 0$ (starred Problem 8.11); take $h \in L(W - D) \setminus L(W - D - P)$, write $h = G/Z^{n-3}$ with $G$ adjoint, so $\operatorname{div}(G) = D + E + A$ with $A \not\geq P$; take a line $L$ through $P$ with $L \cdot C = P + B$; for $f \in L(D + P)$ the residue theorem gives an adjoint $H$ of degree $n - 2$ with $\operatorname{div}(H) = (D' + P) + E + (A + B)$, $D' = \operatorname{div}(f) + D$; Bézout forces $L \subseteq H$, hence $D' \geq 0$. Short route: given Riemann–Roch, $l(D+P) - l(D) = 1 + l(W - D - P) - l(W - D)$ and the last difference is $0$ or $-1$ (Problem 8.29); the hypothesis $l(D) > 0$ is then unnecessary.
>
> Formalization notes: Since Tau Ceti has Riemann–Roch, prove the statement by the short route. The book's proof uses starred Problems 7.21(a) and 8.11. > [!milestone] Riemann–Roch theorem for the nonsingular model of a plane curve
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $C$ an irreducible projective curve of genus $g$ with nonsingular model $X$, and $W$ a canonical divisor. For every divisor $D$ on $X$:
> $$l(D) = \deg D + 1 - g + l(W - D). \qquad (\ast)$$
> Corollaries: $l(W) = g$ and $\deg W = 2g - 2$; $l(D) = \deg D + 1 - g$ if $\deg D \geq 2g - 1$; $l(D - P) = l(D) - 1$ for all $P$ if $\deg D \geq 2g$; (Clifford) if $l(D) > 0$ and $l(W - D) > 0$ then $l(D) \leq \frac{1}{2}\deg D + 1$; $\delta(D) = \dim_k \Omega(D) = l(W - D)$, and the holomorphic differentials form a space of dimension $g$; a divisor $D$ with $\deg D = 2g - 2$ and $l(D) = g$ is canonical; if $D + D' = W$ then $l(D) - l(D') = \frac{1}{2}(\deg D - \deg D')$. Plane form: if $C$ is a plane curve of degree $n \geq 3$ with only ordinary multiple points and $D \geq 0$, then $l(W - D)$ is the dimension of the space of adjoints $G$ of degree $n - 3$ with $\operatorname{div}(G) \geq D + E$ (zero included).
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §8.6, Riemann–Roch Theorem, Corollaries 1–4 and Proposition 9, pp. 108–109], [@Fulton2008, Problems 8.29, 8.30 and 8.31, pp. 109–110]. The plane form is not stated in the book; it follows from Proposition 8 and Corollary 3 of §8.3 and is implicit in the proof of the reduction lemma.
>
> Proof routes: The book's (Brill–Noether): (∗) holds for $\deg D$ large (Riemann's theorem, Corollary 3); the reduction lemma controls how both sides change from $D$ to $D \pm P$; Case 1 ($l(W-D) = 0$) by induction on $l(D)$, Case 2 by a divisor of maximal degree for which (∗) fails. This uses starred Problems 8.8, 8.11 and 8.13. Tau Ceti's route (Weil, Stichtenoth): $L(W-D) \cong \Omega(D)$ via $f \mapsto f\omega$, and $\dim_k \Omega(D)$ is the index of specialty, by duality with repartitions. Clifford: the book embeds $L(D')/L(0)$ into $L(W)/L(D)$ by multiplication by a section with exact poles; Tau Ceti proves $l(A) + l(B) \leq 1 + l(A+B)$ and applies it to $A = D$, $B = W - D$.
>
> Formalization notes: Do not reprove the theorem; Tau Ceti's version is more general (any field with exact constants). The library's task is the bridge of the milestone *Order and divisor of a differential on the nonsingular model*: once the book's canonical divisors are known to represent `TauCeti.canonicalClass`, `TauCeti.isRiemannRochDivisor_of_divisorClass_eq_canonicalClass` gives (∗) for every such $W$. $\Omega(D)$ in the book consists of Kähler differentials; transport it to `TauCeti.weilDifferentialFiltration D` through the comparison. > [!milestone] Independence of adjoint conditions in degree at least n − 3
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $P_1, \dots, P_m \in \mathbb{P}^2$ distinct and $r_1, \dots, r_m \geq 0$. Suppose some irreducible plane curve $C$ of degree $n$ has only ordinary multiple points, all among the $P_i$, with $m_{P_i}(C) = r_i + 1$ for each $i$. Then for every $d \geq n - 3$ the curves of degree $d$ with $m_{P_i} \geq r_i$ for all $i$ form a linear system of dimension exactly
> $$\frac{d(d+3)}{2} - \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{(r_i + 1)r_i}{2}$$
> (dimension $-1$ meaning that the system is empty); equivalently, the space of such forms has dimension $\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{2} - \sum_i \frac{(r_i+1)r_i}{2}$. Compare Theorem 1 of §5.2, which guarantees equality only for $d \geq \sum_i r_i - 1$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, Problem 8.32, p. 110].
>
> Proof routes: These forms are the adjoints $V_d$ of $C$. By the exact sequence for $L(E_d)$, $\dim V_d = \dim W_{d-n} + l(E_d)$. For $d \geq n - 2$, $\deg E_d = dn - \sum_i (r_i+1)r_i > 2g - 2$, so Riemann–Roch gives $l(E_d) = \deg E_d + 1 - g$; for $d = n - 3$, $E_{n-3}$ is canonical and $l(E_{n-3}) = g$. Substituting the genus formula gives the count.
>
> Formalization notes: State it for the vector space of forms (`MvPolynomial.homogeneousSubmodule`), where no $-1$ convention is needed. Dependencies: $L(E_m)$ milestone, genus formula, canonical divisors via adjoints, Riemann–Roch.
>

## Notes on the source

- **Text extraction.** The page was drafted from the text extraction of the PDF; the fact-check compared it with the printed pages (pp. 97–113, rendered from the PDF). The items below say what is printed.
- **Wrong cross-references.** The proof of the Corollary to Proposition 8 (p. 107) cites "Corollary 3 (b) of §8.2" (so printed); the result meant is Corollary 3(b) to Proposition 5, in §8.3. Step (2) of the proof of Riemann's Theorem (p. 101) cites "Propositions 2 and 3 of §8.2"; Proposition 2 is in §8.1.
- **The genus-2 example (p. 104) is wrong as printed.** The printed page gives $Y^2XZ = X^4 + Y^4$, said to have one node and genus 2. This curve has at $[0:0:1]$ the affine equation $XY^2 - X^4 - Y^4 = 0$, a triple point with tangent cone $XY^2$ (not ordinary: the tangent $Y$ is double); in characteristic $\neq 2$ it is irreducible ($Y^4 - XY^2 + X^4$ has no root and no quadratic factor over $k(X)$), so $g \leq g^* = 3 - 3 = 0$ by Corollary 1 to Proposition 5 and the curve is rational. A correct example: in characteristic $\neq 2$, $Y^2XZ = X^4 + Z^4$ has exactly one singular point, a node at $[0:1:0]$ (affine equation $XZ - X^4 - Z^4$); it is irreducible (a reducible quartic has at least three singular points or a non-nodal one), so its genus is $3 - 1 = 2$ by Proposition 5 (checked in the fact-check: $F_Y = 2XYZ$ forces $X = 0$, $Y = 0$ or $Z = 0$, and only $[0:1:0]$ survives). In characteristic 2 it has further singular points (for example $[1:0:1]$). The library should verify whichever example it formalizes.
- **Residue Theorem (p. 98).** The proof does not check that $F \nmid G'$, which is needed for $\operatorname{div}(G')$ to make sense; it follows from $F \nmid GH$ ($F$ is irreducible and divides neither $G$ nor $H$), since $GH = F'F + G'H'$ and $F \mid G'$ would give $F \mid GH$. Proposition 3 of §7.5 covers the multiple points only; at simple points and at points off $C$ Noether's conditions hold for the easier reasons of Chapter 5.
- **Proposition 6 (p. 106), sign slip in the proof.** With $u = -F_X(x,y)/F_Y(x,y)$, the derivation is printed as $D(\bar G) = G_X(x,y) - uG_Y(x,y)$; then $D(\bar F) = 2F_X(x,y) \neq 0$, so it is not well defined. The right formula is $G_X + uG_Y$ (so that $D(x) = 1$, $D(y) = u$, matching $dy = u\,dx$).
- **Proposition 6(1) in characteristic $p$.** The proof's "we may assume $F_Y \neq 0$ (since $F$ is irreducible)" is valid in every characteristic: $F_Y$ vanishes for some models ($F \in k[X, Y^p]$), but then $F_X \neq 0$, since an irreducible $F$ over the perfect field $k$ does not lie in $k[X^p, Y^p] = k[X,Y]^p$; exchange $x$ and $y$. (The drafting agent's claim that the proof fails in characteristic $p$ was wrong.) Only Proposition 6(2) and the definition of $df/dt$ need Appendix A's separating element.
- **Proposition 8 (p. 107).** The last line of the proof prints $\operatorname{ord}_Q(f_x) = r_Q^{-1}$; read $r_Q - 1$. In characteristic $p$ the book's coordinate conditions suffice (see the milestone), because a line meeting $C$ transversally in $n \geq 2$ points forces $F_X \neq 0$ and $F_Y \neq 0$. This is the drafting agent's check, repeated in the fact-check; the book does not say it.
- **Reduction Lemma (p. 108).** The line $L$ with $n - 1$ distinct simple points besides $P$ may not exist in characteristic $p$ when $P$ is a terrible point (Appendix A): the lines through $P$ with $n - 1$ distinct further points form an open subset of the pencil, so if infinitely many lines fail, all do. The argument works with multiplicities, as explained in the milestone (checked in the fact-check: if $H = gL + hF$ locally, then $I(Q, H \cap L) \geq I(Q, C \cap L)$ at each simple point $Q$).
- **Riemann–Roch, Case 2 (p. 109).** "Pick a maximal $D$ for which $(*)_D$ is false" should be read as a divisor of maximal degree among those; it exists because such $D$ have $l(W-D) > 0$, hence $\deg D \leq 2g - 2$.
- **Corollary to Proposition 4 (p. 101)**, item (3), is printed as $\deg((x_0)) = 1$; $\deg((x)_0) = 1$ is meant, as the proof shows.
- **Appendix A (p. 113)**: "then $k = k(x, y)$" should read $K = k(x, y)$.
- **Problem 8.35(b) (p. 110).** The hint is printed "Use a $g^3_4$". With the book's convention ($g^r_n$: $\dim V = r + 1$, Problem 8.33), on a curve of genus 2 every divisor of degree 4 has $l = 4 + 1 - 2 = 3$ (Corollary 2 to Riemann–Roch), so a $g^3_4$ cannot exist; the intended series is a (complete) $g^2_4$, whose map to $\mathbb{P}^2$ gives the quartic.
- **Problem 8.3(b) (p. 99) is false as worded.** "$P + Q \equiv R + S$ iff the line through $P, Q$ meets the line through $R, S$ in a point of $C$" fails when the lines meet at one of the four points: for $R = P$ and $S \neq Q$ (with $P, Q, S$ not collinear) the lines meet at $P \in C$, but $P + Q \equiv P + S$ forces $Q = S$ by (a). The correct condition, used in the milestone, is that the third intersection points of $C$ with the two lines coincide.
- **Problem 8.32 (p. 110)** does not say whether $C$ may have multiple points other than the $P_i$; the count is right when all multiple points of $C$ are among the $P_i$ (points with $r_i = 0$ being simple points of $C$), as stated in the milestone.
- **Sketched proofs.** Proposition 2(1)–(4), the "rest is easy" of the Corollary to Proposition 4, Corollaries 1–3 to Riemann–Roch ("straight-forward"), and the well-definedness check in Lemma 2 are left to the reader. Several results the chapter uses are starred problems: 8.8, 8.11 and 8.13 here; cited directly from earlier chapters, 1.54 and 2.29 (Proposition 4), 2.30 (Proposition 7), 2.49 (Proposition 3), 4.17 (divisors of functions), 5.16 and 7.4 (Proposition 8), 7.19 (adjoints) and 7.21 (Lemma 1, the reduction lemma); and, through Proposition 2 of §7.5 and Problem 7.19, also 2.44, 2.50, 2.53 and 7.5.
- **Characteristic.** See Conventions: with the corrections above, all propositions, lemmas, theorems and corollaries of Chapter 8 hold in every characteristic over an algebraically closed $k$, except Proposition 6(2) as worded (Problems 8.36(b), (c), 8.37 and 8.38 are stated for characteristic 0); Example (2) of §8.3 needs a general Weierstrass equation in characteristic $2$. The fact-check confirmed this result by result for the statements (the function-field results are Tau Ceti theorems over any field with exact constants; Propositions 5 and 8, the residue theorem and the adjoint statements were checked by hand); the characteristic-$p$ existence of plane models with only ordinary multiple points rests on Appendix A's sketch for Theorem 2 of §7.4.
