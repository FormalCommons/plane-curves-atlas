# Local properties of plane curves

This page covers Chapter 3 of Fulton's *Algebraic Curves*, "Local Properties of Plane Curves" [@Fulton2008, Chapter 3, pp. 31–42]:

- §3.1 Multiple points and tangent lines (pp. 31–34): the multiplicity $m_P(F)$ of an affine plane curve at a point, simple and multiple points, tangent lines and their multiplicities, ordinary multiple points.
- §3.2 Multiplicities and local rings (pp. 34–36): a point is simple exactly when $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ is a discrete valuation ring (Theorem 1), and $m_P(F)$ is read off the local ring as $\dim_k \mathfrak m^n/\mathfrak m^{n+1}$ for large $n$ (Theorem 2).
- §3.3 Intersection numbers (pp. 36–41; p. 42 is blank): the local intersection number $I(P, F\cap G)$, its seven defining properties, existence and uniqueness (Theorem 3), the bound $I(P,F\cap G)\ge m_P(F)\,m_P(G)$, and the further Properties (8) and (9).

This chapter is the local core of the library. Chapter 5 defines multiplicities and intersection numbers of projective curves by dehomogenizing to the definitions here, and Bézout's theorem, Noether's theorem, the genus bound and the resolution of singularities all use the results below. The prerequisites (coordinate rings, the local rings $\mathcal{O}_P(V)$, discrete valuation rings, forms, ideals with finitely many zeros, exact sequences and dimension counts) are on the auxiliary page [Affine algebraic sets and varieties](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md).

## Conventions

### The book

- **The field.** The 1969 preface says that $k$ is a fixed algebraically closed field "from Chapter 3 on", and that characteristic zero is assumed "whenever convenient", often without comment in the problems, with Appendix A (p. 113) on arbitrary characteristic. In fact the opening of Chapter 2 (p. 17) already fixes $k$ algebraically closed. Appendix A discusses Proposition 9(2) of §6.5, differentials in Chapter 8, Problems 5.26, 5.28 and 6.47, and Lemma 1 and Theorem 2 of §7.4. It mentions no result of Chapter 3. The text of Chapter 3 never uses characteristic zero. It is used explicitly only in Problem 3.5, and silently in Problem 3.22(b) and in the worked examples (see the notes on the source). **Every milestone on this page holds in arbitrary characteristic**; algebraic closure is needed only where stated.
- **Curves** (§3.1, p. 31). An affine plane curve is a nonconstant $F\in k[X,Y]$ up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar. Multiple factors are allowed. If $F=\prod F_i^{e_i}$ with $F_i$ irreducible, the $F_i$ are the *components* and $e_i$ their *multiplicities*. $V(F)$ recovers the components but not the multiplicities. The degree of the curve is $\deg F$; a line is a curve of degree one. For irreducible $F$ the book writes $\Gamma(F)$, $k(F)$, $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ for the objects attached to the variety $V(F)$.
- **Points.** $P=(a,b)\in\mathbb{A}^2=k^2$. "$P\in F$" means $F(P)=0$.
- **Multiplicity and tangents** (pp. 32–33). Translate $P$ to the origin, $F^T=F(X+a,Y+b)$. Write $F^T=F_m+F_{m+1}+\cdots$ with $F_i$ a form of degree $i$ and $F_m\ne0$. Then $m_P(F)=m$, $F_m$ is the *leading form*, and its linear factors $L_i=\alpha_iX+\beta_iY$, with multiplicity $r_i$, give the tangent lines $\alpha_i(X-a)+\beta_i(Y-b)$. A line through $P$ that is not tangent is a "tangent of multiplicity zero".
- **Notation clash.** The book writes $m_P(F)$ for the multiplicity and the same letters in fraktur, $\mathfrak m_P(F)$, for the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$. A plain-text copy cannot tell them apart. This page writes $\mathfrak m_P$ for maximal ideals. In the uniqueness proof of Theorem 3, $m$ also denotes an unrelated exponent.
- **Numbering.** Theorems are numbered consecutively through the chapter: Theorems 1 and 2 are in §3.2, Theorem 3 in §3.3; the book cites them as "Theorem 2 of §3.2". The nine properties of the intersection number in §3.3 are unnumbered as results and are cited here as "Property ($n$)". Problems are numbered 3.1–3.24 within the chapter; the starred ones are 3.13, 3.19 and 3.24. By the 1969 preface, the results of starred problems are used freely in the text.

### The libraries, and traps

- **Polynomial ring and coordinates.** Use `MvPolynomial (Fin 2) k`, with $X$ = `X 0` and $Y$ = `X 1`, and points `P : Fin 2 → k`. This is what Tau Ceti's plane-curve files use (for example `TauCeti/AlgebraicGeometry/Curves/PlaneCurve/Basic.lean` for projective curves, `TauCeti/Algebra/MvPolynomial/Fin/Two.lean` for bivariate helpers). $F_X$ is `MvPolynomial.pderiv 0 F`. For the uniqueness proof of Theorem 3, `Polynomial (Polynomial k)` (that is $k[X][Y]$, $Y$ outer) is more convenient: $F(X,0)$ is `p.coeff 0`, and $Y\mid F$ iff `p.coeff 0 = 0` (`Polynomial.X_dvd_iff`). Mathlib's `Polynomial.Bivariate.equivMvPolynomial : R[X][Y] ≃ₐ[R] MvPolynomial (Fin 2) R` (`Mathlib/Algebra/Polynomial/Bivariate.lean`) converts between the two.
- **Curves are polynomials, not sets or reduced schemes.** Multiplicities of components enter $m_P$ ($m_P(F^2)=2m_P(F)$) and $I$ (Property (6)). Do not model a curve by `MvPolynomial.zeroLocus` or by a reduced subscheme. Every notion on this page depends only on the principal ideal $(F)$, so no quotient type is needed. State theorems for polynomials, with lemmas such as `MvPolynomial.orderAt_eq_of_associated`. If a curve type is wanted, nonzero non-unit elements of `Associates (MvPolynomial (Fin 2) k)` model it exactly (a free commutative monoid on the irreducible curves). The scheme-theoretic counterpart of a possibly non-reduced curve is `Spec (MvPolynomial (Fin 2) k ⧸ Ideal.span {F})`, like Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.AlgebraicGeometry.projectivePlaneCurve` (`Proj` of $R[X_0,X_1,X_2]/(G)$), which also keeps multiplicities.
- **Constants and zero.** Fulton's curves are nonconstant, but Property (2) mentions nonzero constants, and the uniqueness proof applies the axioms to unit polynomials. Define $m_P$ and $I$ on all polynomials. Convenient conventions: $m_P(0)=\infty$ (Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.orderAt` gives `⊤` exactly at `0`), $m_P(F)=0$ iff $F(P)\ne0$; $I(P, 0\cap G)=\infty$ if $G(P)=0$ and $0$ otherwise (what the length definition gives).
- **"No common component" is not `IsCoprime`.** In $k[X,Y]$, `IsCoprime F G` means $(F,G)=(1)$: over an algebraically closed field, the curves do not meet at all. Fulton's "no common component", and "$F_m$ and $G_n$ have no common factors" in the proof of Property (5), are `IsRelPrime` (`Mathlib/Algebra/Divisibility/Units.lean`) or "`gcd F G` is a unit". "Intersect properly at $P$" (no common component through $P$) is cleanly `eval P (gcd F G) ≠ 0`. This also handles zero polynomials, since $\gcd(F,0)=F$.
- **Intersection numbers take values in $\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}$.** Use `ℕ∞` with `⊤` for $\infty$. `Module.finrank` returns `0` on infinite-dimensional spaces; a definition through `finrank` would make improper intersections have intersection number $0$. Use `Module.length` (valued in `ℕ∞`, `Mathlib/RingTheory/Length.lean`) or `(Module.rank k M).toENat`. In `ℕ∞`, `⊤ + a = ⊤`, and `⊤ * 0 = 0` (`WithTop.top_mul'`), which matters for $m_P(F)\,m_P(G)$ when one factor is $0$ and the other is $\infty$.
- **Dimension versus length.** Fulton defines $I(P,F\cap G)=\dim_k \mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F,G)$. With $\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$ = `Localization.AtPrime (MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal k {P})` (Mathlib provides the `IsMaximal` instance in `Mathlib/RingTheory/Nullstellensatz.lean`), the residue field is $k$, and for every $\mathcal{O}$-module $M$, $\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}}M=\dim_kM$ in `ℕ∞`. This follows from Mathlib's `IsLocalRing.length_restrictScalars` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/LocalRing/Length.lean`, with $A=k$) and `Module.length_eq_rank`. Recommendation: **define** $I(P,F\cap G)$ as `Module.length 𝒪 (𝒪 ⧸ Ideal.span {F, G})` (images of $F,G$ in $\mathcal{O}$), and prove the equality with $\dim_k$ as a lemma. The length is intrinsic, is what Mathlib's `Ring.ord` and Tau Ceti's `RingTheory/Intersection.lean` use, and stays the right local invariant over non-closed fields at non-rational points, where $\dim_k$ is the residue degree times the length.
- **Local rings of curves.** Fulton's $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ (rational functions on $V(F)$ defined at $P$) exists only for irreducible $F$. In Lean it is the localization of $k[X,Y]/(F)$ at the image of the maximal ideal of $P$, which is isomorphic to $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$ (Problem 2.44, starred; see the auxiliary page). For reducible or non-reduced $F$, use $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$, the local ring of $\operatorname{Spec}k[X,Y]/(F)$ at $P$. Theorems 1 and 2 hold in that generality.
- **Multiplicity in Tau Ceti.** `MvPolynomial.taylor a` (the shift $p\mapsto p(X+a)$) and `MvPolynomial.orderAt p a : ℕ∞` (the least degree of a nonzero Taylor coefficient) in `TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/OrderAt.lean` are exactly Fulton's $F^T$ and $m_P(F)$, in any number of variables, over any commutative semiring. `MvPolynomial.mem_pow_vanishingIdeal_singleton_iff` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/LocalOrder.lean`) gives the coordinate-free form $F\in I_P^n\iff n\le m_P(F)$, with $I_P=(X-a,Y-b)$. The library should build on these.
- **Fulton's $F^T$ is contravariant.** $F^T=F(T_1,T_2)$ and $V(F^T)=T^{-1}(V(F))$. Property (3) reads $I(P,F\cap G)=I(Q,F^T\cap G^T)$ when $T(Q)=P$.
- **Tangent directions.** Represent the tangent directions at $P$ as linear forms up to a scalar (points of $\mathbb{P}^1$) dividing the leading form `homogeneousComponent (orderAt F P) (taylor P F)`. Represent tangent multiplicities as the multiplicity of the factor in the unique factorization domain $k[X,Y]$ (`Associates.count`, `multiplicity`). That the leading form is a product of linear forms needs $k$ algebraically closed (the Corollary in §2.6, auxiliary page). Over other fields, "no common tangent" should be phrased as "the leading forms are relatively prime".
- **Mathlib's resultant.** `Polynomial.resultant f g m n` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/Resultant/Basic.lean`) is the determinant of the Sylvester matrix `Polynomial.sylvester`, with sizes defaulting to `natDegree`. Chapter 3 does not use resultants. They are useful in three places. (i) The Bézout identity $AF+BG=D(X)$ behind the finiteness of $V(F,G)$ (§1.6, Proposition 2), on which Property (1) rests: `Polynomial.exists_mul_add_mul_eq_C_resultant` over $R=k[X]$. (ii) Problem 3.21: intersection with a line is root multiplicity after substitution. (iii) The classical alternative definition: in coordinates where no two common zeros of $F,G$ share an $X$-coordinate, $\sum I$ over the points on $X=a$ is $\operatorname{ord}_{X=a}\operatorname{Res}_Y(F,G)$. Do not take (iii) as the definition, because it depends on coordinates and needs general position, but it is a route to Bézout's theorem in Chapter 5. Traps: `Polynomial.resultant_comm` carries the sign $(-1)^{mn}$; resultants commute with specialization (`Polynomial.resultant_map_map`) only for fixed size arguments, so a drop of $\deg_Y$ under $X\mapsto a$ must be tracked; `Polynomial.resultant_eq_zero_iff` is stated over a field, so for $R=k[X]$ pass to $k(X)$.

## Milestones

### §3.1 Multiple points and tangent lines

> [!milestone] Multiplicity and simple points of an affine plane curve
> Let $k$ be a field (the book: algebraically closed, any characteristic), $F\in k[X,Y]$ nonzero and $P=(a,b)\in k^2$. Write $F(X+a,Y+b)=F_m+F_{m+1}+\cdots$ with $F_i$ forms of degree $i$ and $F_m\neq0$, and set $m_P(F)=m$ ($m_P(0)=\infty$). Then:
> 1. $m_P(F)>0$ iff $F(P)=0$; $m_P(\lambda F)=m_P(F)$ for $\lambda\in k^\times$; $m_P(F)\le\deg F$.
> 2. For $P\in F$: $P$ is a simple point ($F_X(P)\ne0$ or $F_Y(P)\ne0$) iff $m_P(F)=1$. In that case $F_1=F_X(P)X+F_Y(P)Y$, so the unique tangent line at $P$ is $F_X(P)(X-a)+F_Y(P)(Y-b)$, and the two definitions of the tangent line at a simple point agree.
> 3. Coordinate-free form: $m_P(F)=\max\{n : F\in I_P^n\}$ with $I_P=(X-a,Y-b)$. Hence, for every affine change of coordinates $T$ with $T(Q)=P$: $m_Q(F^T)=m_P(F)$, and the linear part of $T$ carries the tangent lines of $F^T$ at $Q$ to those of $F$ at $P$, preserving their multiplicities. More generally (Problem 3.8), for a polynomial map $T$ with $T(Q)=P$, $m_Q(F^T)\ge m_P(F)$, with equality when the Jacobian matrix $J_QT$ is invertible. The converse fails: $T=(X^2,Y)$, $F=Y-X^2$, $P=Q=(0,0)$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.1, pp. 31–33]; Problem 3.8 [@Fulton2008, §3.1, p. 34].
>
> Proof routes: The book uses the chain rule ($F_X(P)=F^T_X(0,0)$) and the observation that the linear part of $F^T$ is $F_X(P)X+F_Y(P)Y$. Invariance: an affine change maps $I_Q^n$ onto $I_P^n$. For Problem 3.8(b), the leading form of $F^T$ is the leading form of $F$ evaluated at the linear part of $T$, which is nonzero when that linear part is invertible.
>
> Formalization notes: Define $m_P(F)$ as Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.orderAt F P`, valued in `ℕ∞`, and define the leading form as `homogeneousComponent (orderAt F P).toNat (taylor P F)`. Do not introduce a new multiplicity. Item 1 and part of item 3 are already there; item 2 needs the "iff" packaged from `orderAt_eq_one_of_eval_pderiv_ne_zero` and `homogeneousComponent_one_taylor`. Equality in item 3 for invertible $T$ follows from `orderAt_le_orderAt_aeval` applied to $T$ and $T^{-1}$. The tangent-line statement needs a tangent-cone API, which is missing. Work for any field: nothing in this milestone needs algebraic closure. Problem 3.3 (a curve of degree $n$ with a point of multiplicity $n$ is a union of $n$ lines through it, not necessarily distinct) follows from item 1 and the factorization of binary forms (Corollary in §2.6, [auxiliary page](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md)).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `MvPolynomial.homogeneousComponent`, `MvPolynomial.IsHomogeneous` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/Homogeneous.lean`), `MvPowerSeries.order` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/MvPowerSeries/Order.lean`), `MvPolynomial.pderiv`. Tau Ceti **partial**: in `TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/OrderAt.lean`, `MvPolynomial.taylor`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt`, `orderAt_pos_iff` and `orderAt_eq_zero_iff` (item 1), `orderAt_eq_of_associated`, `orderAt_le_totalDegree`, `orderAt_eq_one_of_eval_pderiv_ne_zero` (simple implies multiplicity one), `homogeneousComponent_one_taylor` (the linear part is the differential), `orderAt_taylor`, `orderAt_le_orderAt_aeval` (Problem 3.8(a), in full), `homogeneousComponent_aeval_of_le_order` (the leading form of a substitution; the core of 3.8(b)); in `TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/LocalOrder.lean`, `MvPolynomial.mem_pow_vanishingIdeal_singleton_iff` (the coordinate-free form). Missing: the packaged "simple iff $m_P=1$", tangent lines and their multiplicities, and equality in Problem 3.8(b).

> [!milestone] Multiplicity and tangent lines of a product of plane curves
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F,G\in k[X,Y]$ nonzero and $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$. Then $m_P(FG)=m_P(F)+m_P(G)$, and the leading form of $FG$ at $P$ is the product of the leading forms. Consequently, if $F=\prod F_i^{e_i}$ is the factorization into irreducible components, then $m_P(F)=\sum_i e_i\,m_P(F_i)$, and a line that is tangent to $F_i$ at $P$ with multiplicity $r_i$ (possibly $0$) is tangent to $F$ with multiplicity $\sum_i e_ir_i$. The leading form of $F$ at $P$ factors as $\prod L_j^{r_j}$ over distinct lines $L_j$ with $\sum r_j=m_P(F)$. In particular, $P$ is a simple point of $F$ iff $P$ lies on exactly one component $F_i$, that component has $e_i=1$, and $P$ is simple on $F_i$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.1, p. 33].
>
> Proof routes: The lowest-degree part of a product is the product of the lowest-degree parts, because $k[X,Y]$ is a domain. The factorization of the leading form into lines is the Corollary in §2.6 (forms in two variables over an algebraically closed field), on the [auxiliary page](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md).
>
> Formalization notes: The multiplicity part holds over any field, indeed any domain: Tau Ceti has it. For leading forms use Mathlib's `MvPowerSeries.homogeneousComponent_mul_of_le_order` through Tau Ceti's coercion lemma `MvPolynomial.coe_homogeneousComponent` (`OrderAt.lean`), or prove the polynomial version directly. Tangent multiplicities are then additive because `Associates.count` of a prime is additive over products. Only the existence of the factorization into lines needs algebraic closure.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `MvPowerSeries.order_mul` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/MvPowerSeries/NoZeroDivisors.lean`), `MvPowerSeries.homogeneousComponent_mul_of_le_order` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/MvPowerSeries/Order.lean`). Tau Ceti **partial**: `MvPolynomial.orderAt_mul`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt_pow`, `MvPolynomial.orderAt_prod` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/OrderAt.lean`) prove the multiplicity statements in full. Leading forms of products, tangent multiplicities and the simple-point corollary are missing.

> [!milestone] Finiteness of the multiple points of a reduced plane curve
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (perfect suffices; any characteristic) and $F\in k[X,Y]$ nonconstant. If $F$ is irreducible, $F$ has only finitely many multiple points. More generally, the same holds if $F$ has no multiple components (is squarefree). Conversely, if $G^2\mid F$ with $G$ nonconstant, then every point of $V(G)$ is a multiple point of $F$. In $\mathbb{A}^n$, $n\ge3$, an irreducible hypersurface can have infinitely many multiple points (Problem 3.10 asks this).
>
> Sources: Problem 3.10 [@Fulton2008, §3.1, p. 34]. The projective version is Problem 5.8, starred, and Problem 7.1 restates it for any curve.
>
> Proof routes: The multiple points are $V(F,F_X,F_Y)$. For irreducible $F$, not both partial derivatives vanish: in characteristic $p$, $F_X=F_Y=0$ forces $F\in k[X^p,Y^p]=k[X,Y]^p$ when $k$ is perfect. Say $F_X\ne0$. Then $F\nmid F_X$ by degree, so $V(F,F_X)$ is finite by Proposition 2 of §1.6 ([auxiliary page](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md)). In the squarefree case, the multiple points are the multiple points of the components together with the pairwise intersections of components, all finite sets. For hypersurfaces in $\mathbb{A}^3$, the surface $X^2-Y^2Z$ is singular along the $Z$-axis.
>
> Formalization notes: Over an imperfect field an irreducible $F$ can have $F_X=F_Y=0$ (for example $X^p-tY^p$ over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, irreducible because $t$ is not a $p$-th power). Then every point of $V(F)$ with coordinates in $\bar k$ is multiple, so the statement for points over $\bar k$ fails; the only point with coordinates in $k$ is the origin. Keep a perfectness or algebraic-closure hypothesis. Express "finitely many" as `Set.Finite {P | IsMultiplePoint F P}`. This is used in Chapter 5 (genus bound, $g^*(C)\ge0$) and Chapter 7.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti **related**: `MvPolynomial.isDedekindDomain_quotient_span_singleton` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/RegularLocalRing/Hypersurface.lean`) treats the case without singular points ($(F,F_X,F_Y)=(1)$); the scheme-theoretic singular locus `AlgebraicGeometry.Scheme.singularLocus` (`TauCeti/AlgebraicGeometry/Curves/SingularLocus/Basic.lean`) carries no finiteness statement.

### §3.2 Multiplicities and local rings

> [!milestone] Simple points of a plane curve are those with a discrete valuation local ring
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F$ an irreducible plane curve and $P\in F$. Then $P$ is a simple point of $F$ iff $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ is a discrete valuation ring. If $P$ is simple and $L=aX+bY+c$ is a line through $P$ that is not tangent to $F$ at $P$, the image of $L$ in $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ is a uniformizing parameter. Write $\operatorname{ord}^F_P$ for the valuation; for a reducible $F$ with $P$ simple, $\operatorname{ord}^F_P$ is the valuation of the unique component through $P$. Then, for every line $L$ through a simple point $P$: $\operatorname{ord}^F_P(L)=1$ if $L$ is not tangent to $F$ at $P$, and $\operatorname{ord}^F_P(L)\ge2$ if it is (with value $\infty$ if $L$ is a component).
> General form: for every $F\in k[X,Y]$ with $F(P)=0$, possibly reducible or non-reduced, $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$ is a DVR iff $m_P(F)=1$. For irreducible $F$ this ring is $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ (Problem 2.44, starred), which gives the book's form.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.2, Theorem 1, p. 34], with the converse completed on p. 36; the order of a line, [@Fulton2008, §3.2, p. 35].
>
> Proof routes: Book: change coordinates so that $P=(0,0)$, the tangent is $Y$ and $L=X$ (Problems 2.15(d) and 2.22, starred). Then $\mathfrak m_P=(x,y)$ (Problems 2.43, 2.44, starred), and writing $F=YG-X^2H$ with $G(0,0)\ne0$ gives $y=x^2h/g\in(x)$. A Noetherian local domain with principal maximal ideal is a DVR (Proposition 4 of §2.5). The converse comes from Theorem 2 together with $\dim_k\mathfrak m^n/\mathfrak m^{n+1}=1$ in a DVR (Problem 2.50, starred). Better for formalization: by Problem 3.13 (milestone below), $\dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=1$ iff $m_P(F)=1$, and Mathlib's `IsLocalRing.finrank_CotangentSpace_eq_one_iff` says that a Noetherian local domain is a DVR iff its cotangent space has dimension $1$. That proves both directions at once. The general form follows the same way once $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$ is known to be a domain when $m_P(F)=1$ (a regular local ring), or via Tau Ceti's Jacobian criterion.
>
> Formalization notes: Work with `Localization.AtPrime (MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal k {P})` modulo $F$, not with rational functions. The uniformizer statement and $\operatorname{ord}(L)$ come from the explicit relation $y=x^2h/g$ after the coordinate change, so this needs the affine-invariance part of the first milestone. Every input of the book's proof is a starred problem of Chapter 2 (2.15, 2.22, 2.43, 2.44, 2.50); those are on the [auxiliary page](../auxiliary/affine-varieties.md). Depends on: [Embedding dimension of a plane curve at a point](#32-multiplicities-and-local-rings) (for the cotangent route) or on the Hilbert–Samuel milestone (for the book's route).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `IsLocalRing.finrank_CotangentSpace_eq_one_iff` and `IsDiscreteValuationRing.TFAE` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/DiscreteValuationRing/TFAE.lean`), `IsDiscreteValuationRing.addVal` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/DiscreteValuationRing/Basic.lean`), `IsRegularLocalRing` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/RegularLocalRing/Defs.lean`). Tau Ceti **partial** (one direction): `Ideal.isRegularLocalRing_localization_of_derivation_notMem` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/RegularLocalRing/Hypersurface.lean`) shows that $(A/(f))_{\mathfrak p}$ is regular when $A_{\mathfrak q}$ is regular and $Df\notin\mathfrak q$ for a derivation $D$. With $A=k[X,Y]$ and $D=\partial/\partial X$ this gives "simple implies regular of dimension one", hence a DVR. `MvPolynomial.isDedekindDomain_quotient_span_singleton` (same file) gives the global form for irreducible $F$ with $(F,F_X,F_Y)=(1)$. `TauCeti.IsRegularLocalRing.parabola_regular_and_cusp_not_regular` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/RegularLocalRing/Parabola.lean`) checks the examples $Y-X^2$ and $Y^2-X^3$. Missing: the converse, the uniformizer, and $\operatorname{ord}^F_P(L)$.

> [!milestone] Multiplicity of a plane curve is determined by its local ring
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F$ an irreducible plane curve, $P\in F$, $\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ and $\mathfrak m$ its maximal ideal. Then $\dim_k\mathfrak m^n/\mathfrak m^{n+1}=m_P(F)$ for all $n\ge m_P(F)$, and $\dim_k\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak m^n=n\,m_P(F)-\tfrac{m_P(F)(m_P(F)-1)}{2}$ for $n\ge m_P(F)$. Hence $m_P(F)$ depends only on the local ring: if $\mathcal{O}_P(F)\cong\mathcal{O}_Q(G)$ as rings, then $m_P(F)=m_Q(G)$, the dimensions being taken over the residue fields.
> General form, combined with Problem 3.13 (not stated in the book; checked here): for every $F\in k[X,Y]$ (reducible, non-reduced, or with $F(P)\ne0$; and $F=0$ with $m_P(0)=\infty$), with $\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$ and $\mathfrak m$ the image of the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$,
> $$\dim_k\mathfrak m^n/\mathfrak m^{n+1}=\min\bigl(n+1,\;m_P(F)\bigr)\quad\text{for all }n\ge0 .$$
> So equality with $m_P(F)$ holds already for $n\ge m_P(F)-1$. Equivalently $\dim_k\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak m^n=n(n+1)/2$ for $n\le m$ and $nm-m(m-1)/2$ for $n\ge m$, where $m=m_P(F)$. When $F(P)\ne0$, $\mathcal{O}$ is the zero ring (not a local ring) and both sides are $0$; in Lean, state the general form for $F(P)=0$, or for the image of the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$ rather than `IsLocalRing.maximalIdeal`.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.2, Theorem 2, p. 35]; the Hilbert–Samuel remark, [@Fulton2008, §3.2, p. 36].
>
> Proof routes: Book: for $n\ge m$, with $I=(X,Y)$, the sequence $0\to k[X,Y]/I^{n-m}\xrightarrow{\cdot F}k[X,Y]/I^n\to k[X,Y]/(I^n,F)\to0$ is exact. Here $\dim k[X,Y]/I^n=n(n+1)/2$ (Problem 2.46, starred), and $k[X,Y]/(I^n,F)\cong\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak m^n$ (Corollary 2 of §2.9 and Problems 2.43, 2.44, starred); then take differences using Problem 2.49(e) (starred) and Proposition 7 of §2.10. Injectivity of $\cdot F$ uses that the lowest form of $FG$ is $F_mG_{\rm low}\ne0$. Alternative, cleaner route: the associated graded ring of $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$ is $k[X,Y]/(F_m)$, with $F_m$ the leading form. This holds because $\operatorname{gr}\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)=k[X,Y]$ is a domain, so the initial ideal of $(F)$ is $(F_m)$. The degree-$n$ part of $k[X,Y]/(F_m)$ has dimension $\min(n+1,m)$.
>
> Formalization notes: State the general form first; the book's form and Problem 3.13 follow. Use lengths over $\mathcal{O}$ with `Module.length_eq_add_of_exact` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/Length.lean`) instead of $\dim_k$, or Mathlib's `IsLocalRing.CotangentSpace` for $n=1$. Chapter 5 uses this result to define $m_P$ for projective curves independently of the chart.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: no Hilbert–Samuel function of a local ring; `Polynomial.hilbertPoly` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/HilbertPoly.lean`) concerns graded modules. Tau Ceti **related**: `MvPolynomial.orderAt_span_singleton_pointToPoint` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/MvPolynomial/LocalOrder.lean`; the order of $(F)$ in $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$ is $m_P(F)$, so $m_P(F)$ depends only on $(F)\subset\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$, a weaker statement), `TauCeti.IsRegularLocalRing.associatedGradedEquiv`.

> [!milestone] Embedding dimension of a plane curve at a point
> (Problem 3.13, starred.) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F$ an irreducible plane curve, $P\in F$, $\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_P(F)$ with maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, and $m=m_P(F)$. Then $\dim_k\mathfrak m^n/\mathfrak m^{n+1}=n+1$ for $0\le n<m$. In particular $\dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=1$ if $P$ is a simple point and $\dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2=2$ otherwise. ### §3.3 Intersection numbers

> [!milestone] Finiteness and vanishing of the local intersection number
> Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic). For $F,G\in k[X,Y]$ and $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$ set $I(P,F\cap G)=\dim_k\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F,G)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}$. Then:
> - (Property (1)) $I(P,F\cap G)<\infty$ iff $F$ and $G$ intersect properly at $P$, that is, have no common component passing through $P$, that is, $\gcd(F,G)(P)\ne0$. This also covers zero polynomials: $I(P,0\cap G)=\infty$ iff $G(P)=0$.
> - (Property (2)) $I(P,F\cap G)=0$ iff $F(P)\ne0$ or $G(P)\ne0$. $I(P,F\cap G)$ depends only on the components through $P$: $I(P,FU\cap G)=I(P,F\cap G)$ whenever $U(P)\ne0$. In particular $I(P,F\cap G)=0$ when $F$ or $G$ is a nonzero constant.
> - (Property (3)) For an affine change of coordinates $T$ with $T(Q)=P$: $I(P,F\cap G)=I(Q,F^T\cap G^T)$.
> - (Property (4)) $I(P,F\cap G)=I(P,G\cap F)$.
> - (Property (7)) $I(P,F\cap(G+AF))=I(P,F\cap G)$ for all $A\in k[X,Y]$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Properties (1)–(4), (7), pp. 36–37, and the proof of existence in Theorem 3, pp. 37–38].
>
> Proof routes: (2), (4), (7) hold because $I$ depends only on the ideal $(F,G)\mathcal{O}$. (3) holds because $T$ induces $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)\cong\mathcal{O}_Q(\mathbb{A}^2)$ (Problem 2.22, starred). (1): if there is no common component through $P$, the ideal $(F,G)$ has finitely many zeros and Corollary 1 of §2.9 gives finiteness; equivalently, $(F,G)\mathcal{O}$ is $\mathfrak m$-primary. If $H$ is a common component through $P$, then $\mathcal{O}/(F,G)$ surjects onto $\mathcal{O}/(H)\cong\mathcal{O}_P(H)\supset\Gamma(H)$, which is infinite-dimensional (Corollary 4 to the Nullstellensatz). Length route: $\mathcal{O}/(H)$ has Krull dimension one, hence infinite length.
>
> Formalization notes: Define $I$ as a length, as recommended in the conventions, and prove `= (Module.rank k _).toENat`. For (1), prove $\sqrt{(F,G)\mathcal{O}}=\mathfrak m$ under properness (from the finiteness of $V(F,G)$ for curves with no common component, §1.6 Proposition 2, and the Nullstellensatz `MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal_zeroLocus_eq_radical`), then apply Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.exists_nat_length_quotient_span_pair`. For the converse, apply `TauCeti.length_self_eq_top_of_ringKrullDim_pos` to $\mathcal{O}/(H)$. For (3), use Tau Ceti's `MvPolynomial.taylor` for translations and an `AlgEquiv` of `MvPolynomial (Fin 2) k` for linear changes. Avoid `finrank` (see the conventions).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `Module.length`, `Module.length_ne_top_iff` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/Length.lean`), `IsLocalRing.length_restrictScalars`, `MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal`. Tau Ceti **partial**: for an arbitrary commutative ring $R$, `TauCeti.length_quotient_span_pair_eq_zero_iff`, `TauCeti.length_quotient_span_pair_eq_zero_iff_isUnit` (vanishing, Property (2)) and `TauCeti.length_quotient_span_pair_comm` (Property (4)); for a local ring, `TauCeti.one_le_length_quotient_span_pair`; for a Noetherian local ring, `TauCeti.exists_nat_length_quotient_span_pair` (finiteness from an $\mathfrak m$-primary pair), `TauCeti.isFiniteLength_quotient_span_pair_of_prime` (finiteness for prime $(f)$ in a 2-dimensional local ring), all in `TauCeti/RingTheory/Intersection.lean`; `Ideal.isFiniteLength_quotient_of_isMaximal_radical` and `TauCeti.length_self_eq_top_of_ringKrullDim_pos` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/Length.lean`). > [!milestone] Additivity of local intersection numbers
> (Property (6).) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic). For all $F,G,H\in k[X,Y]$ and $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$, in $\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}$,
> $$I(P,F\cap GH)=I(P,F\cap G)+I(P,F\cap H).$$
> Hence, if $F=\prod F_i^{r_i}$ and $G=\prod G_j^{s_j}$, then $I(P,F\cap G)=\sum_{i,j}r_is_j\,I(P,F_i\cap G_j)$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Property (6), p. 37, and its proof, p. 38].
>
> Proof routes: If $F$ and $GH$ have a common component through $P$, both sides are $\infty$: the component is prime, so it divides $G$ or $H$. Otherwise the sequence $0\to\mathcal{O}/(F,H)\xrightarrow{\cdot G}\mathcal{O}/(F,GH)\to\mathcal{O}/(F,G)\to0$ is exact. The book proves injectivity by clearing denominators and using that $F$ and $G$ have no common factor, and leaves the rest to the reader. In length language: the image of $G$ in $\mathcal{O}/(F)$ is a non-zero-divisor, and $\operatorname{length}\mathcal{O}/(F,g)=\operatorname{ord}_{\mathcal{O}/(F)}(g)$ is additive (Mathlib's `Ring.ord_mul`).
>
> Formalization notes: Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.length_quotient_span_pair_mul_eq_add_of_mem_nonZeroDivisors` is exactly this exact-sequence argument for an arbitrary commutative ring, additive in the second argument when the multiplier is a non-zero-divisor on $R/(f)$. The library must show: if $F$ and $H$ (or $G$) have no common component through $P$, the image is a non-zero-divisor on $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$. The zero divisors there lie in the union of the ideals $(F_i)$ of the components through $P$, because $k[X,Y]$ is a unique factorization domain. Then handle the improper case by the previous milestone. Symmetry transports additivity to the first argument. State the bilinear form; the book's factorized form is a corollary by induction (`Finset.sum`, `Associates.factors`). Uniqueness uses additivity with a constant factor, so include $F$ or $G$ constant.
>

> [!milestone] Powers of the maximal ideal in the ideal of two curves without common tangents
> (The Lemma, part (a), in the proof of Theorem 3.) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F,G\in k[X,Y]$ nonzero, $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$, $m=m_P(F)$, $n=m_P(G)$, $I_P=(X-a,Y-b)$, and suppose $F$ and $G$ have no tangent line in common at $P$. Then $I_P^t\,\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)\subset(F,G)\,\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$ for all $t\ge m+n-1$. If $m=0$ or $n=0$, the right side is the whole ring.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Lemma (a) in the proof of Theorem 3, p. 39]. Used in Chapter 5 in the proof of the local conditions for Noether's theorem, which cites "the lemma used to prove Property (5)".
>
> Proof routes: Book: with $L_1,\dots,L_m$ and $M_1,\dots,M_n$ the tangents (with repetition), the products $A_{ij}=L_1\cdots L_iM_1\cdots M_j$ with $i+j=t$ form a basis of the forms of degree $t$ (Problem 2.35(c), starred, which needs no $L_i$ proportional to an $M_j$). For $i+j\ge m+n-1$ either $i\ge m$ or $j\ge n$, and then $A_{ij}\in(F,G)+I_P^{t+1}$. Separately, $I_P^N\subset(F,G)\mathcal{O}$ for large $N$, by the Nullstellensatz. Descending induction concludes. Route that works over any field: if the leading forms $F_m,G_n$ are relatively prime, every form of degree $t\ge m+n-1$ lies in $(F_m,G_n)$. This is a dimension count: the map $(A,B)\mapsto AF_m+BG_n$ from forms of degrees $t-m$ and $t-n$ has kernel $\{(G_nC,-F_mC)\}$ of dimension $t-m-n+1$, hence image of dimension $t+1$. It is the homogeneous Sylvester map.
>

> [!milestone] Intersection number is at least the product of the multiplicities
> (Property (5).) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F,G\in k[X,Y]$ nonzero and $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$. Then $I(P,F\cap G)\ge m_P(F)\,m_P(G)$, with equality iff $F$ and $G$ have no tangent line in common at $P$. In particular $I(P,F\cap G)=1$ iff $F$ and $G$ meet transversally at $P$ ($P$ simple on both, with distinct tangent lines). Degenerate cases: if $P\notin F$ both sides are $0$ and $F$ has no tangents at $P$; if $F,G$ share a component through $P$, then $I=\infty$ and they share a tangent.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Property (5), p. 37; proof pp. 38–39, with the Lemma, p. 39].
>

> [!milestone] Characterization of the local intersection number by Fulton's seven properties
> (Theorem 3.) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic). There is a unique function assigning to every $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$ and all $F,G\in k[X,Y]$ (nonzero; Fulton says "all plane curves", but the properties involve nonzero constants) a value $I(P,F\cap G)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}$ satisfying Properties (1)–(7):
> 1. finite iff $F,G$ intersect properly at $P$;
> 2. zero iff $P\notin F\cap G$, depends only on the components through $P$, and is zero if $F$ or $G$ is a nonzero constant;
> 3. invariance under affine coordinate changes;
> 4. symmetry;
> 5. $\ge m_P(F)m_P(G)$, with equality iff there is no common tangent;
> 6. additivity over factorizations;
> 7. $I(P,F\cap G)=I(P,F\cap(G+AF))$.
>
> It is $I(P,F\cap G)=\dim_k\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F,G)$. Stronger uniqueness: any $J$ satisfying (1), (2), invariance under translations, (4), (6), (7) and $J((0,0),X\cap Y)=1$ equals $I$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Theorem 3, p. 37; uniqueness p. 37; existence pp. 37–39; the remark on redundancy, pp. 39–40].
>
> Proof routes: Existence is the conjunction of the four preceding milestones. Uniqueness by Fulton's procedure. Translate $P$ to $(0,0)$. If $J=\infty$, then $I=\infty$ by (1). Otherwise argue by induction on $n=J(P,F\cap G)$, with $n=0$ given by (2). Let $r=\deg F(X,0)$ and $s=\deg G(X,0)$ (taken to be $0$ for the zero polynomial), with $r\le s$ by (4).
> - Case $r=0$: $F(X,0)=0$ since $F(0,0)=0$, so $F=YH$. By (6), $J(F\cap G)=J(Y\cap G)+J(H\cap G)$. Write $G(X,0)=X^{m}u(X)$ with $u(0)\ne0$. Here $G(X,0)\ne0$, since otherwise $Y$ would be a common component and $J$ would be infinite; and $m>0$. Then $J(Y\cap G)=m$ by (7), (6), (2) and $J(Y\cap X)=1$, so $J(H\cap G)<n$ and induction applies.
> - Case $r>0$: replace $G$ by $G-cX^{s-r}F$, with $c$ the ratio of leading coefficients. This keeps $J$ by (7) and lowers $s$.
>
> The inner induction is on $r+s$. The procedure is also an algorithm: the book's Example (p. 40) uses it to compute $I((0,0),E\cap F)=14$ for the quartic $E$ and the sextic $F$ of §3.1.
>
> Formalization notes: Package the axioms as a `structure` on functions `(Fin 2 → k) → MvPolynomial (Fin 2) k → MvPolynomial (Fin 2) k → ℕ∞`. Prove the faithful seven-axiom theorem and the stronger minimal-hypothesis uniqueness; only the latter is used later. The full Property (5) needs the tangent-line API merely to be stated. Induction: prove `∀ n : ℕ, ∀ F G, I P F G = n → J P F G = n` by `Nat.strong_induction_on` on the value of the concrete $I$ (already shown to satisfy the axioms, hence finite exactly when $J$ is). Inside, use strong induction on $r+s$, generalizing $F,G$. Do the Euclidean step in $k[X][Y]$ through `Polynomial.Bivariate.equivMvPolynomial`, where $F(X,0)$ is `coeff 0` and natural degrees satisfy `natDegree 0 = 0`, matching Fulton's convention. Using the leading-coefficient ratio $c$ avoids the book's normalization to monic polynomials, which would need scaling invariance (derivable from (2) and (6)). Then $J(Y\cap G)=m$ comes from $G-G(X,0)\in(Y)$ and (7), followed by (6) on $X^m\cdot u$ and (2) on $u$, a unit at $P$ that may be a constant. The procedure can also be implemented as a computable function on $\mathbb{Q}[X,Y]$ and checked against the definition on the book's examples.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. > [!milestone] Intersection number at a simple point is an order of vanishing
> (Property (8).) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $P$ a simple point of $F$ (so exactly one component $F_1$ of $F$ passes through $P$, and it is simple), and $G\in k[X,Y]$. Then $I(P,F\cap G)=\operatorname{ord}^F_P(G)$, the order in the DVR $\mathcal{O}_P(F_1)$ of the image of $G$, which is $\infty$ iff $F_1\mid G$. Consequence (Problem 3.20): $I(P,F\cap(G+H))\ge\min\bigl(I(P,F\cap G),I(P,F\cap H)\bigr)$ at a simple point. This fails at a non-simple point: for $F=XY$, $G=X+Y^3$, $H=Y+X^3$ at $(0,0)$, $I(F\cap G)=I(F\cap H)=4$ but $I(F\cap(G+H))=2$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Property (8), p. 40]; Problems 3.18 and 3.20 [@Fulton2008, §3.3, p. 40].
>
> Proof routes: Book: reduce to $F$ irreducible by (2) and (6). Then $\operatorname{ord}(g)=\dim_k\mathcal{O}_P(F)/(g)$ (Problem 2.50(c), starred) and $\mathcal{O}_P(F)/(g)\cong\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F,G)$ (Problem 2.44, starred). Problem 3.18 asks for a proof from (1)–(7) only: after a coordinate change $F=Y+\cdots$ is a graph locally, and (7) replaces $G$ by $G(X,\varphi(X))$ modulo $F$. The length route is cleaner and needs no axioms.
>
> Formalization notes: This is nearly a composition of library lemmas. Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.ord_eq_length_quotient_span_pair` gives $\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{O}/(F,G)=$ `Ring.ord (𝒪 ⧸ (F)) g`, and Mathlib's `Ring.ord_eq_addVal` identifies `Ring.ord` with the valuation in a DVR. Theorem 1 supplies the DVR. Problem 3.20 is then Mathlib's `Ring.ord_add`. Define $\operatorname{ord}^F_P$ for reducible $F$ through the general form of Theorem 1 ($\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$ is itself the DVR when $P$ is simple), which removes the need to pick the component.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `Ring.ord_eq_addVal`, `Ring.ord_add` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/OrderOfVanishing/Noetherian.lean`), `IsDiscreteValuationRing.addVal`. Tau Ceti **partial**: `TauCeti.ord_eq_length_quotient_span_pair` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/Intersection.lean`) is the algebraic identity behind Property (8) in any commutative ring; the plane-curve statement needs Theorem 1.

> [!milestone] Sum of local intersection numbers equals the dimension of the intersection ring
> (Property (9).) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic) and $F,G\in k[X,Y]$ with no common component. Then $V(F,G)$ is finite and
> $$\sum_{P\in\mathbb{A}^2}I(P,F\cap G)=\dim_k k[X,Y]/(F,G)<\infty .$$
> If $F,G$ have a nonconstant common factor $H$, both sides are $\infty$: $H$ has a zero $P$ (algebraic closure), where $I(P,F\cap G)=\infty$, and $k[X,Y]/(F,G)$ surjects onto the infinite-dimensional $k[X,Y]/(H)$.
>
> Sources: [@Fulton2008, §3.3, Property (9), p. 40].
>
> Proof routes: Corollary 1 of §2.9 (the Chinese-remainder decomposition $k[X,Y]/I\cong\prod_P\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/I\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)$ for an ideal $I$ with finitely many zeros; auxiliary page). In Mathlib terms: $A=k[X,Y]/(F,G)$ is a finite-dimensional, hence Artinian, $k$-algebra; Mathlib's `IsArtinianRing.finrank_eq_sum_primeSpectrum` gives $\dim_kA=\sum_{\mathfrak p}\dim_kA_{\mathfrak p}$. The primes of $A$ are the maximal ideals of the points of $V(F,G)$ (Nullstellensatz), and $A_{\mathfrak p}\cong\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F,G)$ (localization commutes with quotients).
>
> Formalization notes: Sum over `(V(F,G)).toFinset`, or use `finsum` over all $P$ (the summands vanish off $V(F,G)$). Finite-dimensionality of $A$ is the auxiliary page's §2.9 milestone, or follows from the finiteness of $V(F,G)$ and the Nullstellensatz. This is the input for Bézout's theorem in Chapter 5.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **partial**: `IsArtinianRing.finrank_eq_sum_primeSpectrum` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/Spectrum/Prime/Noetherian.lean`) and `PrimeSpectrum.toPiLocalizationEquiv` (`Mathlib/RingTheory/Spectrum/Prime/Topology.lean`) give the algebraic decomposition for any finite-dimensional algebra; the identification of primes with points and of the local factors with $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F,G)$ is missing. Tau Ceti **related**: `TauCeti.AlgebraicGeometry.LineBundleClass.intersectionNumber_inv_mk_toInvertibleSheaf_eq_finsum_of_isClosedImmersion` (`TauCeti/AlgebraicGeometry/IntersectionNumber/Basic.lean`), a global sum-of-local-lengths formula for a Cartier divisor and a proper curve.

> [!milestone] Tangency of a line detected by the intersection number
> (Problem 3.19, starred.) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F\in k[X,Y]$ nonzero, $P\in\mathbb{A}^2$, and $L$ a line through $P$. Then $L$ is tangent to $F$ at $P$ iff $I(P,F\cap L)>m_P(F)$. If $L$ is a component of $F$, then $I=\infty$ and $L$ is tangent. If $P\notin F$, then $I=0=m_P(F)$ and $L$ is a tangent of multiplicity zero, that is, not tangent.
>
> Sources: Problem 3.19 [@Fulton2008, §3.3, p. 40]. Chapter 5 takes this as the definition of tangency for projective curves.
>
> Proof routes: Property (5) with $m_P(L)=1$, the only tangent of $L$ being $L$ itself.
>
> Formalization notes: An immediate corollary of [Intersection number is at least the product of the multiplicities](#33-intersection-numbers); state it in the form Chapter 5 needs, with "tangent" meaning that the linear form divides the leading form at $P$. Also used in Problem 3.24.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib —. Tau Ceti —.

> [!milestone] Intersection of a plane curve with a line
> (Problem 3.21.) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F\in k[X,Y]$, and $L=\{(a+tb,\,c+td): t\in k\}$ a line, $(b,d)\ne(0,0)$, that is not a component of $F$. Put $G(T)=F(a+Tb,c+Td)\in k[T]$, which is nonzero, and factor $G=\varepsilon\prod_i(T-\lambda_i)^{e_i}$ with distinct $\lambda_i$. Then $\lambda_i\mapsto P_i=(a+\lambda_ib,c+\lambda_id)$ is a bijection onto $L\cap V(F)$, and $I(P_i,L\cap F)=e_i$. Consequently $\sum_{P}I(P,L\cap F)=\deg G\le\deg F$.
>
> Sources: Problem 3.21 [@Fulton2008, §3.3, p. 40].
>
> Proof routes: Take coordinates in which $L=Y$, a simple curve with $\mathcal{O}_P(L)\cong k[T]_{(T-\lambda)}$. By Property (8), $I(P,L\cap F)=\operatorname{ord}_{T=\lambda}G=$ root multiplicity. Alternatively, by (7) and (6): $I(P,Y\cap F)=I(P,Y\cap F(X,0))$, and $F(X,0)$ factors into linear factors in $X$. The degree bound: $\deg G\le\deg F$, and the number of roots with multiplicity is $\deg G$ because $k$ is algebraically closed.
>
> Formalization notes: State with `Polynomial.rootMultiplicity` (`Mathlib/Algebra/Polynomial/Div.lean`) of the substituted polynomial `MvPolynomial.aeval ![C a + C b * X, C c + C d * X] F`. Equality with $\deg F$ fails exactly when the direction $(b,d)$ is a zero of the top-degree form of $F$ (a point at infinity of $F$ on $L$), which is the content of Bézout for lines in Chapter 5. In the resultant picture, $\operatorname{Res}_T$ against a linear polynomial is evaluation (`Polynomial.resultant_X_sub_C_right`), but this problem needs no resultant.
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `Polynomial.rootMultiplicity`, `Polynomial.card_roots'` (`Mathlib/Algebra/Polynomial/Roots.lean`). Tau Ceti —.

> [!milestone] Local-ring characterization of ordinary multiple points
> (Problem 3.24, starred.) Let $k$ be algebraically closed (any characteristic), $F$ an irreducible plane curve, $P=(0,0)\in F$ with $m=m_P(F)>1$, $\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_P(F)$, $\mathfrak m$ its maximal ideal, and $\bar g$ the residue in $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ of $g\in\mathfrak m$.
> - (a) $aX+bY\mapsto\overline{ax+by}$ is an isomorphism from the linear forms onto $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$.
> - (b) If $P$ is an ordinary multiple point with tangents $L_1,\dots,L_m$, then $I(P,F\cap L_i)>m$ and the residues $\bar l_i$ are pairwise non-proportional (and nonzero).
> - (c) Conversely, if $G_1,\dots,G_m\in k[X,Y]$ satisfy $I(P,F\cap G_i)>m$, with residues $\bar g_i\in\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ nonzero and pairwise non-proportional, then $P$ is an ordinary multiple point.
> - (d) Hence: $P$ is an ordinary multiple point iff there are $g_1,\dots,g_m\in\mathfrak m$ with nonzero, pairwise non-proportional residues in $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ and $\dim_k\mathcal{O}/(g_i)>m$ for all $i$. So being an ordinary multiple point depends only on $\mathcal{O}_P(F)$.
>
> Sources: Problem 3.24 [@Fulton2008, §3.3, p. 41].
>
> Proof routes: (a) is Problem 3.13 in degree one. (b) Problem 3.19 and (a). (c) As in the book's hint: $\bar g_i\ne0$ means $G_i=L_i+{}$higher terms with $L_i\ne0$, so $m_P(G_i)=1$ with tangent $L_i$; then $I(P,F\cap G_i)>m=m_P(F)\,m_P(G_i)$ forces $L_i$ to be tangent to $F$ by Property (5); the $L_i$ are distinct by (a), so $F$ has $m$ distinct tangents. (d) combines (b) and (c) with $\dim_k\mathcal{O}/(g_i)=I(P,F\cap G_i)$ (Problem 2.44, starred).
>
> Formalization notes: The printed conditions "$\bar l_i\ne\lambda\bar l_j$" and "$\bar g_i\ne\lambda\bar g_j$" are about residues in $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ (the bars are lost in the text extraction `CurveBook.txt`), with $\lambda=0$ allowed, so the residues are nonzero. State (d) for an abstract local ring isomorphism: if $\mathcal{O}_P(F)\cong\mathcal{O}_Q(G)$, then $P$ is ordinary on $F$ iff $Q$ is ordinary on $G$. That is how Chapter 7 uses it (multiple points are preserved under the isomorphism $C'\cap U\cong C\cap U$ of a quadratic transformation). The irreducibility of $F$ can be dropped by using $\mathcal{O}_P(\mathbb{A}^2)/(F)$. Depends on [Embedding dimension of a plane curve at a point](#32-multiplicities-and-local-rings) and [Intersection number is at least the product of the multiplicities](#33-intersection-numbers).
>
> Lean status: Mathlib **related**: `IsLocalRing.CotangentSpace`. Tau Ceti **related**: `TauCeti.isUnit_det_hessian_iff_isUnit_discrim` (`TauCeti/RingTheory/LocalRing/Hessian.lean`) and the node files under `TauCeti/RingTheory/Node/` treat ordinary double points (nodes) in a scheme-theoretic, Fitting-ideal formulation.

## Notes on the source

1. **Where $k$ becomes algebraically closed.** The preface says "from Chapter 3 on", but Chapter 2 opens (p. 17) by fixing $k$ algebraically closed. Nothing in Chapter 3 needs more than that, and nothing in it needs characteristic zero. Appendix A lists no Chapter 3 result.
2. **Curves versus polynomials in Theorem 3.** Theorem 3 speaks of "all plane curves $F,G$", which are nonconstant by §3.1. Yet Property (2) prescribes the value on nonzero constants, and the uniqueness proof applies (2) and (6) to $u(X)$ with $u(0)\ne0$, which may be constant, and normalizes $F(X,0)$ to be monic by scaling (free for curves, which are classes up to scalars). The axioms must be read on all nonzero polynomials; scaling invariance then has to be derived from (2) and (6).
3. **Tacit step in Case 1 of the uniqueness proof** (p. 37). The proof writes $G(X,0)=X^m(a_0+a_1X+\cdots)$ with $a_0\ne0$, tacitly assuming $G(X,0)\ne0$. This holds: $r=0$ and $F(0,0)=0$ force $F(X,0)=0$, so $Y\mid F$; if also $G(X,0)=0$ then $Y\mid G$, $Y$ is a common component through $P$, and $I=\infty$, which was excluded at the start. The step is correct but unstated.
4. **Index typo in the proof of Lemma (b)** (p. 39, checked on the printed page). With $\psi(A,B)=AF+BG$ on $k[X,Y]/I^n\times k[X,Y]/I^m$, where $m=m_P(F)$, $n=m_P(G)$, $r=\operatorname{ord}A$ and $s=\operatorname{ord}B$, the printed text says "Suppose $r<m$ or $s<n$". Since $A$ is taken modulo $I^n$ and $B$ modulo $I^m$, "$(\bar A,\bar B)\ne0$" means "$r<n$ or $s<m$", and that is what the argument needs: it makes $\min(r+m,s+n)<m+n$, which forces $r+m=s+n$ and $A_rF_m=-B_sG_n$, and it is what the conclusion "$s\ge m$, $r\ge n$" contradicts.
5. **Theorem 2's range.** "For all sufficiently large $n$" is proved for $n\ge m_P(F)$; the equality actually holds for $n\ge m_P(F)-1$, and $\dim\mathfrak m^n/\mathfrak m^{n+1}=\min(n+1,m_P(F))$ for all $n$ (with Problem 3.13).
6. **Characteristic-dependent statements.**
   - Problem 3.5 assumes characteristic zero explicitly. In characteristic $p$, $X^p$ has multiplicity $p$ at the origin but all its ordinary partial derivatives vanish there; Hasse derivatives repair this.
   - Problem 3.22(b) ("cusp iff $F_{XXX}(P)\ne0$") is false in characteristics 2 and 3: $F_{XXX}(P)=6a_{30}$ vanishes identically, yet $Y^2-X^3$ has a cusp in every characteristic. The coefficient criterion "$a_{30}\ne0$" is characteristic-free.
   - Some worked examples fail in small characteristic. The curve $D=Y^2-X^3-X^2$ has a node only in characteristic $\ne2$. The curve $E$ has an ordinary triple point only in characteristic $\ne2,3$: in characteristic 3 its lowest form is $-Y^3$, in characteristic 2 it is $Y(X+Y)^2$. In characteristic 2, moreover, $E=(X+Y)^2(X^2+Y^2+Y)$ and $F=(X+Y)^6$, so $E$ is reducible, $P$ is not the only multiple point of $E$ or $F$ (Problem 3.1 fails), and $I(P,E\cap F)=\infty$.
   - The Example on p. 40 ($I(P,E\cap F)=14$) is correct exactly in characteristic $\ne2,5$. Its steps $I(P,E\cap Y)=4$ and the identities $F-(X^2+Y^2)E=YG$, $G+3E=YH$ hold in every characteristic; the last step $I(P,E\cap H)=m_P(E)\,m_P(H)=6$ needs the lowest forms $Y(3X^2-Y^2)$ of $E$ and $5X^2-3Y^2$ of $H$ to have no common factor. They share the factor $Y$ in characteristic 5 (where $I(P,E\cap F)\ge15$) and $X+Y$ in characteristic 2; in characteristic 3 they are $-Y^3$ and $2X^2$, and $14$ is still right.
   - Problem 3.4 is fine in every characteristic.
7. **Residue bars in Problem 3.24.** The printed conditions "$\bar l_i\ne\lambda\bar l_j$" and "$\bar g_i\ne\lambda\bar g_j$" concern residues in $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$; the bars are lost only in the text extraction, not in the book. With $\lambda=0$ allowed, the conditions also force the residues to be nonzero.
8. **Minor typos in problems.**
   - Problem 3.9(c): $P=(0,0)$ should be the origin of $\mathbb{A}^3$.
   - Problem 3.16: "$F\in k[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ define a hypersurface in $\mathbb{A}^r$" should have $r$ variables, and $P=(0,0)$ should be the origin of $\mathbb{A}^r$.
   - Property (5) reads "have not tangent lines in common".
9. **Proofs left to the reader.**
   - Exactness of the sequence in the proof of (6), apart from injectivity.
   - The "easy to check" exactness of the top row in the proof of (5).
   - The equality of the two tangent-line definitions at simple points.
   - Problems 3.13, 3.19 and 3.24 (starred, used later).
10. **Problem 3.20 counterexample.** The book asks for an example at a non-simple point. One is $F=XY$, $G=X+Y^3$, $H=Y+X^3$ at the origin: by (6) and (7), $I(F\cap G)=3+1$, $I(F\cap H)=1+3$, $I(F\cap(G+H))=1+1$, since $G+H=X(1+X^2)+Y(1+Y^2)$. This example is not from the book; it was checked by hand and holds in every characteristic.
11. **Problem 3.10 over other fields.** The book's $k$ is algebraically closed, and there the statement is right. Over an imperfect field it fails for points with coordinates in $\bar k$: $X^p-tY^p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ with $F_X=F_Y=0$, so all its points over $\bar k$ are multiple (its only point with coordinates in $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is the origin). A perfect $k$ suffices.

## Deferred

- **Problems 3.1, 3.2, 3.14, 3.17 and the Example on p. 40** are computations on specific curves. They are not milestones, but are good regression tests for the definitions (for instance $I((0,0),E\cap F)=14$ in characteristic 0, or any characteristic other than 2 and 5).
- **Problem 3.7** (polar equations, real pictures over $\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}$): analytic and pictorial, outside the algebraic library.
- **Hilbert–Samuel theory beyond plane curves** (the remark on p. 36, Problems 3.15(b) and 3.16, and the closing question of 3.16 on a definition of multiplicity for local rings): this is general commutative algebra of Samuel multiplicity, of which the library needs only the plane-curve case above. If Mathlib or Tau Ceti later gains Hilbert–Samuel functions, the general form of Theorem 2 should be restated through them.
- **Problems 3.6, 3.12, 3.22, 3.23** (constructions, flexes, cusps, hypercusps) are definitions and exercises. Flexes are needed in Chapter 5 and belong with that page's flex and Hessian milestones. Keep the definitions characteristic-free (coefficients instead of derivatives).
